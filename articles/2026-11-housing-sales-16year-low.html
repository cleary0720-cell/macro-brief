The 7% Wall: October Home Sales Just Touched a 16-Year Low

The existing home sales market clocked in at 3.75 million annualized in October — the worst pace since August 2010 — as 7.28% mortgage rates and a median price above $416,000 have frozen out buyers at every income level. With a Fed rate hike now 97% priced in for December 9, the thaw is nowhere in sight.

Written by AI agents designed and managed by Connor Leary · The Macro Brief November 22, 2026
October 2026 existing home sales fell to 3.75 million annualized — their lowest level since August 2010. — Editorial photo

The American housing market has not been this quiet since the aftermath of the financial crisis. The National Association of Realtors reported this month that existing home sales fell to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.75 million in October — a decline of 5.5% from September and 8.8% from a year ago, touching their lowest reading since August 2010. The culprit is a combination most buyers cannot escape: a 30-year fixed mortgage rate sitting at 7.28% as of the latest Freddie Mac survey, and a median existing home price of $416,700 that has now risen year-over-year for 40 consecutive months.

The arithmetic is brutal. A buyer putting 20% down on a median-priced home today faces a monthly principal-and-interest payment of roughly $2,275 — before property taxes, insurance, or maintenance. Redfin estimates the total monthly cost of a typical home purchase at approximately $2,675, requiring an annual household income of around $111,000 to stay within standard debt-to-income guidelines. The U.S. median household income is closer to $78,000. The gap between what buying requires and what most families earn has rarely been wider outside the brief peak of 2023.

The National Association of Realtors' Housing Affordability Index — a composite measure (an index reading of 100 means a family earning the median income has exactly the qualifying income for a median-priced home, with nothing left over) — stood at 100.7 in October, down from 116.5 at the start of this year. The index has deteriorated for five consecutive months. The cushion for the typical American family has effectively evaporated.

First-Time Buyers at the Breaking Point

Perhaps no group has absorbed more punishment than first-time buyers — those without an existing home's equity to deploy. The November 2026 NAR Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers found that first-time purchasers accounted for just 22% of all transactions, near historical lows. An earlier NAR generational trends survey earlier this year found the share had fallen to 21%, the lowest recorded since NAR began tracking the metric in 1981. Without equity from a prior sale — or a family transfer of wealth — clearing the down payment hurdle on a $416,700 home requires saving roughly $83,000 in cash before closing costs. At the median household income, that takes years of near-total savings discipline, years during which rent consumes the very dollars that might otherwise compound toward ownership.

The lock-in effect that has starved the existing home market of supply since 2022 — roughly 85% of outstanding mortgages carry rates below 6%, and owners are simply not selling — has not eased. Inventory rose a modest 1.3% in October to 1.59 million units, a 5.1-month supply at current sales pace, but that figure reflects not new listings so much as the shrinking denominator: homes are sitting longer on the market (a median of 35 days, up from 33 in September) because fewer buyers qualify.

Builders Are Desperate, Permits Are Signaling Retreat

New construction, which briefly provided a release valve when resale supply evaporated, is now straining under the same weight. The National Association of Home Builders' Housing Market Index — a monthly survey of builder confidence — fell to 29 in October, its lowest reading since June 2012. An index below 50 indicates that more builders view conditions as poor than good; a reading of 29 represents near-crisis pessimism.

The NAHB survey found 65% of builders offered sales incentives in October — the 19th consecutive month in which the share has been 60% or higher. Some 43% cut prices outright, with an average reduction of 6%. Industry analysts estimate that roughly 80–90% of new home closings now include some form of mortgage rate buydown, where the builder effectively subsidizes a below-market rate for the buyer's first several years.

Housing starts surprised to the upside in October, rising 9.9% month-over-month to a 1.309 million annualized rate, but the forward-looking permit data told a more troubling story. Total permits fell 7.4% to 1.295 million annualized. Single-family permits — the clearest indicator of what builders actually intend to build in the coming months — dropped to 772,000, their lowest reading since January 2023. Starts often move on project completions already in the pipeline; permits signal the intentions of builders who have just looked at their order books and decided to slow down.

The December Wildcard

The Federal Reserve meets on December 9. CME FedWatch is currently pricing approximately a 97% probability of a 25-basis-point hike, which would raise the federal funds target range to 4.00–4.25%, the highest level since 2007. The October consumer price index, released November 10, reinforced the case for action: the monthly reading came in at 0.4%, above expectations, with shelter, food, and energy all contributing. Chair Jerome Warsh has signaled repeatedly that the Committee will not declare victory on inflation prematurely.

The mortgage market tends to price Fed policy with a lag and a premium. If the December hike proceeds as expected, economists at several major banks project the 30-year fixed rate could drift toward 7.5% by year-end — a threshold that, in combination with current price levels, would push the NAR affordability index below 100 for the first time since 2023, meaning the median household could no longer technically qualify for a median-priced home under standard underwriting rules. The housing market, already at its quietest since the financial crisis, may be one more rate decision away from a new chapter of contraction.

Bottom Line

Existing home sales fell to 3.75 million annualized in October, a 16-year low, as 7.28% mortgage rates and a $416,700 median price have made ownership unaffordable for most American families. First-time buyers have nearly vanished from the market, builders are cutting prices and offering rate concessions on nearly every transaction, and forward permit data suggests the construction pipeline is beginning to slow. With a Fed hike now nearly certain on December 9, the conditions for a meaningful recovery in housing activity remain absent heading into 2027.
