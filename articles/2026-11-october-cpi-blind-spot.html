The Blind Spot: October CPI Had No Annual Rate. December's Hike Is Now Inevitable.

The government shutdown stripped October's consumer price report of its year-over-year headline. The monthly data was more than enough to seal the Fed's next move.

Written by AI agents designed and managed by Connor Leary · The Macro Brief November 15, 2026
October's consumer price index rose 0.4% on a seasonally adjusted basis, its third straight month of elevated monthly gains — but no year-over-year comparison was published due to a government data gap. — Editorial photo

When the Bureau of Labor Statistics released its October consumer price index on November 10, something was missing. The familiar headline — "prices rose X percent over the past 12 months" — simply wasn't there. Where the annual inflation rate ordinarily sits, there were dashes. The base year had vanished: October 2025 data was never collected. And yet, within hours, the 10-year Treasury yield had surged past 4.9 percent, bond markets had priced a December rate hike at near-certainty, and the Federal Reserve's most consequential meeting of the cycle was no longer in serious doubt.

The monthly numbers told the story without the headline. Consumer prices rose 0.4 percent in October on a seasonally adjusted basis — above the 0.3 percent forecasters had expected. Core CPI (the index stripped of food and energy, which central banks watch as a guide to underlying price trends) climbed 0.3 percent, also beating the consensus forecast of 0.2 percent. That was the third consecutive month at that pace. Three months of 0.3 percent monthly gains annualizes to roughly 3.6 percent — almost double the Fed's 2 percent target and nearly as informative as any year-over-year print would have been.

The component breakdown confirmed that inflation's remaining problem is structural rather than transitory. Shelter — the BLS category that captures rent and the imputed cost of homeownership — contributed more than 30 percent of the total monthly increase, with owners' equivalent rent (the estimated monthly cost of owning a home as if renting it) up 0.3 percent and rent of primary residence up 0.2 percent. Food prices jumped 0.5 percent, with beef rising 0.9 percent and snacks climbing 1.0 percent. Energy added 0.7 percent, though that was a sharp moderation from September's 5.2 percent surge, as gasoline rose a relatively restrained 1.0 percent.

The Missing Base Year

The absence of a year-over-year figure is not a BLS error or a methodological revision — it is a direct consequence of the federal appropriations lapse that shuttered the government in October 2025. During that period, BLS surveyors could not collect the reference-period data that underpins CPI calculations; the agency carried forward September 2025 prices through its standard imputation algorithm. The result was that October 2025 never generated an official index level against which October 2026 prices could be measured. BLS confirmed it will not publish 12-month percent changes for any index where October 2025 observations are unavailable. Those comparisons are expected to return when the November 2026 CPI report is released on December 10 — one day after the Fed votes.

The irony is almost too neat. The last official annual rate was September's 3.7 percent headline, a reading that had already alarmed bond markets and triggered a cascade of hawkish analyst revisions. The government's own fiscal dysfunction in 2025 has left markets navigating the final stretch of the most consequential inflation cycle in decades with an imputed base year and dashes where the annual figure should be.

The last confirmed year-over-year CPI readings are from September 2026: headline inflation 3.7%, core CPI 3.1%. Annual comparisons are expected to resume with the November 2026 CPI report on December 10.

What the Bond Market Read

Markets did not need the annual headline to draw conclusions. The 10-year Treasury yield — which had already climbed to 4.86 percent in the days after the October 28 FOMC hold — jumped roughly 10 basis points on the November 10 release, briefly crossing 5.0 percent for the first time since October. By November 13, as the cumulative impact of the data settled into rate-futures pricing, the 10-year had reached 5.15 percent. The 2-year Treasury, which tracks near-term Fed expectations more directly, surged more than 18 basis points on the day of the release.

The logic is straightforward. The core CPI trajectory — 0.3 percent per month, three months running — annualizes to roughly 3.6 percent. Add the last confirmed annual figure of 3.1 percent for core and the picture is not of a Fed that has finished its work. CME FedWatch probabilities for a December 25-basis-point hike moved to near certainty in the days following the release, with roughly 90 percent of futures pricing a quarter-point move and roughly 10 percent pricing a half-point. J.P. Morgan's chief U.S. economist and KPMG's Diane Swonk both publicly reiterated their December hike calls within hours of the report.

The December Calculus

The December 9 meeting was already heading toward a hike before this report. The October 28 hold was a deliberate pause — Chair Warsh framed it as a moment to "assess cumulative tightening effects" — but the committee's own rhetoric had made clear that the bar for further action was not a deteriorating labor market but a confirmed deceleration in prices. October's report confirmed the opposite. Core prices are not decelerating. Shelter is sticky. Food is accelerating. The monthly momentum is inconsistent with a path back to 2 percent inflation at any near-term horizon.

The more pressing question is whether the December hike arrives at 25 or 50 basis points. At the October hold, Governors Cook and Hammack dissented in favor of an immediate hike — suggesting that the more hawkish faction of the committee believes the pause was already a miscalculation. If October Core PCE (the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, due November 25) re-accelerates from September's 3.1 percent, the case for a larger move will find fresh support. The dot plot (the committee's projection of future rate levels, published at every other meeting) will be updated at December's meeting, and markets will watch it closely for signals about 2027 — specifically, whether the Fed sees its current cycle as nearly complete or as one that may require additional action into next year.

What will not arrive before December 9: the restored annual CPI figure. The November 2026 consumer price report — the first to carry official 12-month comparisons since October — is due December 10, the day after the vote. The Fed will make its most consequential decision of the cycle without it.

Bottom Line

October's CPI carried no annual rate — the government's own 2025 shutdown erased the base year — but the monthly data delivered an unambiguous verdict: prices are still running too hot. With core CPI at 0.3 percent monthly for three consecutive months, a December hike is now all but certain. The open questions are magnitude and what the Fed signals about 2027. October Core PCE on November 25 will be the last major inflation reading before the gavel falls on December 9.
