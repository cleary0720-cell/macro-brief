Monetary Policy
Federal Funds Rate
3.88%
HIKED TO 3.75–4.00% ↑
The FOMC voted 12-0 on September 16 to raise the federal funds target by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00% — the first rate hike since the tightening cycle that ended in 2023, and the highest level since 2024. Chair Warsh, citing inflation "too high for too long," made explicit that the Fed sees the current policy stance as insufficiently restrictive. The September dot plot (updated for the first time since June) raised the median year-end 2026 projection to 4.10% — signaling one additional 25-basis-point hike before December. 16 of 18 FOMC participants see at least one more increase; four see two more. The 2027 median climbed to 4.10% from 3.60% in June, a stark upward revision reflecting the Fed’s loss of confidence in near-term disinflation. CME FedWatch now prices the October 27–28 FOMC at 57% hike / 43% hold. The August retail sales surge (+6.0% YoY, released September 16) removed the last argument for pausing: consumers are spending freely even at multi-year-high borrowing costs, which removes the demand-destruction mechanism the hold camp was counting on.
In plain termsThe Fed hiked rates on September 16 — the first increase in three years — pushing the target to 3.75–4.00%, its highest level since 2024. The unanimous 12-0 vote, combined with a dot plot pointing to one more hike before year-end, signals the Fed is back in tightening mode. For your wallet: credit card APRs (already above 22%) rise within one to two billing cycles; adjustable-rate mortgages reset higher this autumn; new car loans above 7.5–8.0%. The 30-year fixed mortgage is now running at approximately 7.0–7.2%, meaning a $400,000 home loan costs roughly $2,695–$2,740 per month. A second hike to 4.00–4.25% at the October 28 meeting (57% probability) would push 30-year rates toward 7.4–7.6%, adding another $60–80 per month to that same loan. If you have variable-rate debt, now is the time to evaluate locking in fixed rates.

For consumers, 3.4% inflation means everyday prices are rising well above the Fed's 2% target, grocery and fuel costs remain elevated, and higher borrowing rates now make credit cards, car loans, and mortgages more expensive. The next inflation checkpoint — August Core PCE on approximately September 26 — will determine whether a second hike at the October 28 FOMC meeting is coming.

The August 2026 employment situation (BLS, released September 5) showed a resilient labor market: nonfarm payrolls surged +162,000 (vs. +53,000 expected) and the unemployment rate held at 4.1%. The BLS revised July from −23,000 to +21,000, erasing the "first job loss in years" scare. The labor market proved strong enough to give the FOMC unanimous cover to hike 25 basis points on September 16 (to 3.75–4.00%). Post-hike, the labor market's next test is the September jobs report (due October 2): with the Fed now tightening, the key question is whether higher borrowing costs begin to cool hiring in Q4. That surprise drop signals the labor market remains healthy even after the hike. If the October 2 September report matches or exceeds August's pace, the case for a second hike at the October 27–28 FOMC (currently at 57% probability) becomes difficult to deny.

If you're looking for work, the labor market remains healthy by historical standards. The more immediate news is that weekly unemployment filings just hit 196,000 — a three-month low — meaning companies are still not laying workers off despite higher interest rates. The next test is the September jobs report (October 2): if hiring stays strong, the Fed will likely hike again at the October 28 meeting. For workers, a resilient job market generally means better wage negotiating power, but the flip side is that persistent employment strength gives the Fed reason to keep raising rates, which raises the cost of every loan, mortgage, and credit card balance.

The picture has become more nuanced since the advance estimate: August payrolls surged (+162,000), August retail sales rebounded strongly (+6.0% YoY, +1.2% monthly, released September 16), and ISM Manufacturing PMI held at 54.6% in August (eighth consecutive expansion month). These data points confirm Q3 is tracking above Q2, likely in the 2.0–2.5% range based on early indicators. The primary new risk is the September 16 rate hike to 3.75–4.00% — tighter financial conditions typically weigh on growth with a 6–12 month lag, suggesting the growth impact of this hike cycle will be felt more fully in Q1–Q2 2027. The Q3 2026 advance GDP estimate is expected in late October. With unemployment at 4.1%, manufacturing expanding, and consumers spending at a +6.0% YoY pace, a near-term recession remains unlikely but the deceleration trend (from +2.1% in Q1 to +1.5% in Q2) bears monitoring.
In plain termsThe economy grew at +1.5% in Q2 2026 — positive, but slower than Q1's +2.1%. Since then, the data has been encouraging: August added +162,000 jobs, retail sales surged +6.0% in August, and factories are still expanding. Q3 is looking like it could come in around 2%+, which would be better than Q2. The risk is that the Fed just raised rates to 3.75–4.00% — and higher rates slow borrowing, spending, and hiring over time, usually with a 6–12 month delay. So while the economy looks healthy right now, the full impact of 2026's tightening cycle will show up more in 2027. A recession requires two consecutive negative quarters — we're nowhere near that threshold yet. Q3 GDP won't be officially confirmed until late October.

REBOUNDING STRONGLY ↑
August 2026 retail sales surged +6.0% year-over-year and +1.2% month-over-month (Census Bureau, released September 16) — a decisive reversal of July's -0.6% monthly decline and a significant beat against the 0.8% monthly consensus. Total August retail and food-services sales reached $773.9 billion. Released simultaneously with the FOMC's 25-basis-point hike on September 16, the August retail data removed the final argument for pausing: the American consumer is spending confidently even into a tightening cycle. The strong August print also complicates the case for a pause at the October 27–28 FOMC (currently 57% hike probability): if consumers remain resilient despite higher rates, the Fed has less reason to stop. September retail sales (due mid-October) will reveal whether the August surge was durable or a one-month seasonal pop.
In plain termsAmericans spent +1.2% more in August than in July — a strong rebound after July's modest decline — and retail sales are now +6.0% above where they were a year ago. This is good news for the economy: consumers are still opening their wallets despite higher prices and borrowing costs. The back-to-school season drove big gains in electronics and clothing. The concern for rate watchers is that strong consumer spending is exactly what keeps inflation alive — and it gives the Fed more reason to hike again at the October 28 meeting. For households, the data suggests the job market and wages are healthy enough to sustain spending, but this also means the Fed is unlikely to reverse course anytime soon. If spending stays strong through Q4, expect rates to remain elevated well into 2027.

M2 grew 5.4% year-over-year through July 2026, per the Federal Reserve's H.6 release published August 25 — a slight moderation from June's 5.5%. Total M2 stands at $23.2 trillion. The sequential easing from May's 5.6% peak continues, but the pace remains gradual. Importantly, the Fed's September 16 rate hike to 3.75–4.00% is now actively working to slow M2 growth through a direct channel: higher rates constrain credit creation, reduce borrowing, and incentivize savings over spending, all of which tend to slow money supply growth with a 6–12 month lag. If the October 28 hike also materializes (57% probability), the M2 deceleration could accelerate into 2027. The August H.6 (due approximately September 22) will reveal whether the July consumer resilience translated into continued money supply growth or whether tighter credit conditions are already beginning to bite. Historically, M2 at 5.4% remains well above the 3–4% range associated with 2% inflation — the structural floor remains intact for now.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money in the economy — cash, bank accounts, and money market funds. At 5.4% annual growth, the money supply is still expanding faster than the economy's real output, which is part of why prices remain above the Fed's 2% target. The good news: the September 16 rate hike to 3.75–4.00% makes borrowing more expensive, which tends to slow money creation as fewer people take out loans. That "money supply brake" effect typically shows up 6–12 months later — meaning today's hike should help pull M2 toward a more disinflation-compatible 3–4% growth range by mid-2027. Until then, the excess liquidity from prior years keeps some upward price pressure in the system.

July 2026 Core PCE — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — held at 3.3% year-over-year for the second consecutive month (BEA, Personal Income and Outlays, released August 26). Markets had expected a decline toward 3.1–3.2%. Two consecutive flat readings at 3.3% formed a critical pillar of the FOMC's justification for the September 16 hike (25 bps, unanimous, target now 3.75–4.00%). The dot plot released alongside the hike raised the 2026 year-end median to 4.10%, and 16 of 18 participants see at least one more increase before December. The August Core PCE (due approximately September 26) is now the most important data point on the horizon: it is the first post-hike inflation read and the primary arbiter of whether the October 27–28 FOMC (57% hike probability) delivers a second consecutive increase. A third straight 3.3% reading would all but seal October tightening; a meaningful decline toward 3.0–3.1% — consistent with Core CPI's 2.4% trajectory — would give the hold camp credible ammunition. The 90-basis-point divergence between Core CPI (2.4%) and Core PCE (3.3%) narrows the uncertainty window: the Fed is watching its own gauge, not the CPI, for the go/no-go signal.
In plain termsCore PCE is the Fed's favorite inflation measure — it strips out food and energy to capture the underlying trend. At 3.3% for July (unchanged from June), inflation is still 1.3 percentage points above the Fed's 2% target, and no progress has been made in two months. This flat reading was a key reason the Fed hiked on September 16. Now, the August Core PCE (due around September 26) is the pivotal next release: if it stays at 3.3%, the Fed is very likely to hike again on October 28. If it drops meaningfully toward 3.0%, there's a real argument to pause. For consumers: the 2% target means the Fed wants annual price increases of only $1 on every $50 you spend, rather than the current $1.65 on every $50. At 3.3%, prices are still running at more than one-and-a-half times the Fed's target. That gap — and whether it's closing — determines whether borrowing costs keep rising this fall.

The August 2026 expansion at 54.6, combined with the August jobs surge (+162,000) and retail sales rebound (+6.0% YoY), argues that the manufacturing sector enters the post-hike period from a position of strength rather than fragility. In plain termsAny ISM reading above 50 means manufacturing is expanding; below 50 means it's shrinking. At 54.6, U.S. factories are in solid growth — the eighth straight month above 50 — with active hiring (employment 51.2), strong new orders (53.7), and robust production (58.3). This is the most important manufacturing data ahead of the October 1 September report, which will be the first post-hike read: did the September 16 rate increase start to cool factory demand, or is the expansion durable? Higher rates make it more expensive for businesses to finance equipment and inventory, so a reading below 52 in October would be an early warning sign. For now, manufacturing is one of the stronger parts of the economy — providing jobs, producing goods, and keeping the industrial economy far from recession territory.

Editor's Analysis

The First Hike in Three Years: Fed Raises to 3.75–4.00% Unanimously, Dot Plot Signals One More, October 28 Becomes the Decisive Meeting

FOMC September 16: 25 bps hike, 12-0 vote; dot-plot median 4.10% for 2026; August retail sales +6.0% YoY; jobless claims 196K (3-month low) — By Connor Leary, September 20, 2026

The Federal Open Market Committee voted 12-0 on September 16 to raise the federal funds target by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%, ending a multi-month hold and delivering the first rate hike since the 2022–2023 tightening cycle. The unanimity is the most important part of the decision. Every voting member — including those who might have leaned toward a pause given the August CPI's mixed signals — aligned with Chair Warsh's view that two months of Core PCE stuck at 3.3% and headline inflation flat at 3.4% constitute insufficient progress toward the 2% target. The dissent-free vote removes any ambiguity about the FOMC's direction: there is no significant internal faction arguing for a pause.

The September dot plot, updated for the first time since June, was more hawkish than markets expected on the forward path. The median 2026 year-end projection rose to 4.10% — implying one additional 25-basis-point hike before December — while the 2027 median climbed from 3.60% to 4.10%, a dramatic upward revision that signals the Fed does not anticipate material disinflation over the next 12 months. 16 of 18 participants see at least one more increase this year; four see two more. CME FedWatch responded by pricing the October 27–28 FOMC at 57% hike / 43% hold — a genuine coin flip that will be resolved by three critical data releases: August Core PCE (approximately September 26), the September jobs report (October 2), and September CPI (approximately October 10).

"The 12-0 vote removed any doubt about direction. The dot plot's 4.10% median means the Fed intends to keep going — the question is only whether October 28 or December carries the next move."

The August retail sales report, released simultaneously with the FOMC decision on September 16, eliminated the last argument for pausing. Sales surged +6.0% year-over-year and +1.2% month-over-month — a decisive reversal of July's modest decline and a significant beat against the 0.8% monthly consensus. The strength was broad: electronics and appliances up 7.8% YoY, back-to-school spending robust, motor vehicle sales running at a 16.8 million annualized pace. A consumer spending at this pace into a tightening cycle does not argue for accommodation. If anything, the August retail data strengthens the case for October — active spending keeps aggregate demand elevated, giving inflation structural support that rate increases are designed to remove.

The labor market sent an equally hawkish signal. Initial jobless claims for the week ending September 12 — released September 17, one day after the hike — fell to 196,000, a three-month low. The 4-week moving average dropped to approximately 203,000, breaking below the 205,000 floor that had held most of 2026. The improvement arrived exactly when it was least expected given the September 16 rate change, and it underscores a critical dynamic: the U.S. labor market has thus far demonstrated remarkable resilience to the cumulative tightening in financial conditions over the past 18 months. Rate hikes typically begin to slow hiring with a 6–9 month lag — meaning the full labor market impact of 2026's tightening is still ahead, not behind us.

The risk that bears careful watching over the next six weeks is the divergence between Core CPI (2.4% in August, a five-year low) and Core PCE (3.3% in July, flat for two months). The Fed's own gauge — Core PCE — has stalled while the BLS measure has improved dramatically. If August Core PCE (due approximately September 26) follows CPI's trajectory and prints near 3.0–3.1%, the hold camp gains credible ammunition for October. If it stays at 3.3% for a third consecutive month, the dot-plot median of 4.10% becomes self-fulfilling: the Fed will hike again. The divergence is explained structurally — PCE weights healthcare and financial services more heavily than CPI, and both categories remain elevated — but the market will interpret the September 26 print as the go/no-go signal for October 28.

For households, the September 16 hike has immediate and tangible consequences. Credit card APRs — already averaging above 22% — will move higher within one to two billing cycles. Adjustable-rate mortgage holders will see resets at the next scheduled adjustment. New car loan rates, already running above 7.5%, will increase further. Fixed 30-year mortgage rates are currently approximately 7.0–7.2%, down slightly from the 7.4–7.6% range that preceded the hike — a "sell the rumor, buy the news" dynamic in the bond market. If the October 28 FOMC delivers a second consecutive hike to 4.00–4.25%, 30-year rates would likely return to the 7.4–7.6% range, adding $60–100 per month to a $400,000 loan. The window for locking in current rates ahead of the next potential move is September 26 through October 2.
