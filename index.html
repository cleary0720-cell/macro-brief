Monetary Policy
Federal Funds Rate
3.88%
HOLD LIKELY: OCT ODDS 18% HIKE →
The FOMC voted 12-0 on September 16 to raise the federal funds target by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00% — the first rate hike since the tightening cycle that ended in 2023, and the highest level since 2024. The week ending October 2 delivered three successive data releases that dramatically reversed rate hike expectations for October 27–28. First, August Core PCE (BEA, released September 30) surprised to the downside at 3.0% — a 30-basis-point decline from two consecutive months at 3.3% and below every major estimate. BEA annual benchmark revisions simultaneously lowered July Core PCE from 3.3% to 3.0%, meaning inflation has been running cooler than previously measured. Second, September ISM Manufacturing PMI (ISM, released October 1) held at 54.5 — ninth consecutive expansion — but the Prices Paid sub-index surged to 77.9, a supply-chain inflation risk. Third, the September employment situation (BLS, released October 2) showed nonfarm payrolls increased just 29,000 — far below the 84,000 consensus — and combined revisions subtracted 60,000 from July (now -10,000) and August (now +133,000). The unemployment rate rose to 4.2%. These three prints collectively moved CME FedWatch from 76% hike / 24% hold on September 26 to 80% hold / 18% hike / 2% cut as of October 3. The Fed hiked into weaker ground than the data initially showed. December 2026 is now the next live meeting for tightening, with hike probabilities there exceeding 60% on FedWatch. Two key inputs arrive before October 28: September CPI (October 14) and the Q3 2026 GDP advance estimate (October 29).
In plain termsThe Fed raised rates on September 16 to 3.75–4.00% — but three data releases this past week pushed October 28 hike odds from 76% down to just 18%. Core PCE fell to 3.0% (bigger drop than expected), the September jobs report showed only 29,000 new positions (well below the 84,000 forecast), and unemployment ticked up to 4.2%. For your wallet: the 30-year fixed mortgage averaged 7.28% as of October 1 (Freddie Mac PMMS) — the highest of 2026, up 25 basis points from 7.03% one week earlier. A $400,000 loan now costs roughly $2,740 per month in principal and interest. The shift to an 80% hold probability means mortgage rates may stabilize or ease: if the Fed pauses October 28 (the likeliest outcome), the 30-year rate could drift back toward 7.0–7.1% as bond markets relax on the improved inflation data. December is now the next likely hike date if CPI remains elevated. Credit card APRs and car loan rates remain elevated but would hold steady if the Fed holds. The September CPI release on October 14 is the next major catalyst.

This PCE improvement — combined with a September jobs miss (+29,000 payrolls, 4.2% unemployment) — drove CME FedWatch October 28 hike odds from 76% to 18%. In plain termsInflation held at 3.4% in August — no improvement for the second straight month. The Fed cited this as direct justification for raising rates on September 16. Core CPI (which strips out food and energy) fell to 2.4% — its lowest level since March 2021 — showing the underlying trend is improving. The Fed's own preferred measure, Core PCE, has now also come in better than expected: August Core PCE arrived at 3.0% on September 30, a meaningful improvement from the 3.3% that had been stuck for two months. Labor Market
Unemployment Rate
4.2%
SLOWING: +29K SEP PAYROLLS ↓
The September 2026 employment situation (BLS, released October 2) delivered a significant downside surprise: nonfarm payrolls increased just 29,000 — far below the 84,000 consensus — while the unemployment rate rose to 4.2% from 4.1%. The report was compounded by substantial revisions: July was revised from +21,000 to -10,000 (down 31,000) and August was revised from +162,000 to +133,000 (down 29,000), subtracting a combined 60,000 jobs from previously reported totals. The three-month average for July through September is now +50,667 — well below the 2025 pace and consistent with a meaningfully decelerating labor market. Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1% month-over-month and 3.0% year-over-year — the slowest annual wage growth of 2026 and below current CPI inflation (3.4%), meaning real wages turned negative again in September. The participation rate rose to 61.8% (a four-month high), partially explaining the unemployment rate increase as new entrants searched for work. Initial jobless claims for the week ending September 26 remained historically low at 197,000 weekly (4-week avg: 200,000) — meaning companies are not yet actively laying workers off, but they have materially slowed new hiring. The September payroll weakness — amplified by the -60,000 in prior revisions — drove CME FedWatch's October 28 hike probability from 76% to 18%. The Fed's September 16 hike, made while August's +162,000 was the standing estimate, now appears more aggressive relative to the revised +133,000 figure. The October 2026 jobs report (November 6) will indicate whether September's weakness is an isolated miss or the beginning of a deceleration trend.
In plain termsThe September jobs report shocked markets: only 29,000 new positions were added (vs. 84,000 forecast), unemployment rose to 4.2%, and prior months were revised down a combined 60,000 jobs — July is now officially a monthly loss (-10,000). For workers currently employed, the picture is still stable: only 200,000 people per week are filing for first-time unemployment benefits (near 57-year lows), meaning companies aren't laying off in large numbers. But hiring has slowed sharply. If you're looking for work, expect fewer openings and more competition. Wage growth at 3.0% is now trailing inflation at 3.4%, meaning paychecks are buying slightly less in real terms. The silver lining: this weak report makes a Fed rate hike on October 28 far less likely (18% probability, down from 76% last week). A pause means borrowing costs — including mortgages at 7.28% and credit card APRs above 22% — may represent the near-term ceiling rather than a floor.

The week's yield dynamics were driven by two competing forces: fiscal and term premium pressures pushing long yields higher (the 10-year and 20-year reached multi-decade highs), while deteriorating rate expectations pulled short-term yields lower following the Core PCE surprise (3.0% on September 30) and jobs miss (+29,000 on October 2). The full yield curve as of October 2: 1M=3.94%, 3M=4.10%, 6M=4.29%, 1Y=4.46%, 2Y=4.84%, 5Y=5.06%, 7Y=5.17%, 10Y=5.28%, 20Y=5.68%, 30Y=5.61%. The 2s10s spread widened to +44 bps (from +26 bps on September 26) and the 3m10y gap expanded to +118 bps — the curve is firmly normal and steepening, a signal that the market is pricing a slower Fed pace (short end falling) into a structurally higher long-end environment. The 20-year Treasury at 5.68% is the highest point on the curve, reflecting elevated fiscal deficit and debt ceiling concerns. The 30-year fixed mortgage hit 7.28% as of October 1 (Freddie Mac PMMS) — the highest of 2026 — before September's jobs miss began to ease market pressure.

The good news: yields fell back after the September jobs report (+29,000 payrolls, well below expectations) and Core PCE cooling to 3.0% reduced pressure on the Fed to hike again. At 5.28%, the 30-year fixed mortgage averaged 7.28% as of October 1 (Freddie Mac PMMS) — the highest of 2026. A $400,000 home loan now costs roughly $2,740 per month. With October hike odds now at just 18%, yields and mortgages could ease: if the Fed holds on October 28 (80% probable), the 10-year may pull back toward 5.0–5.1% and 30-year mortgages could drift back toward 7.0–7.1% over the following weeks. For homebuyers: rates are elevated, but the improved inflation outlook means the ceiling may be close. The 2-year Treasury at 4.84% reflects the reduced urgency of near-term Fed action; the widening 2s10s spread (+44 bps) signals the market expects future easing well before long-term rate pressures subside.

M2 grew 5.7% year-over-year through August 2026, per the Federal Reserve's H.6 release published September 22 — an uptick from July's 5.4% and the first acceleration in M2 growth since May's 5.6% local peak. Total M2 now stands at $23.3 trillion. The rebound reverses a three-month moderating trend and arrives at an uncomfortable moment: the Fed hiked to 3.75–4.00% on September 16 specifically to slow money supply growth and tighten financial conditions. The August acceleration reflected the consumer surge (retail sales +6.0% YoY) translating into higher credit card balances and short-term borrowing. The September jobs report's weakness (+29,000 payrolls, unemployment 4.2%) may reduce credit demand going forward — suggesting September and October M2 data could moderate. With October 28 hike odds now at just 18% (down from 76%), the credit-contraction impulse is less aggressive than previously priced. Historically, M2 above 5.5% is incompatible with the Fed's 2% inflation target at any sustained level. The September H.6 (due approximately October 22) will provide the first post-jobs-report read on whether monetary conditions are tightening as intended.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money in the economy — cash, bank accounts, and money market funds. At 5.7% annual growth in August, the money supply re-accelerated after three months of modest easing — unwelcome news in a week when the Fed is already fighting inflation. The jump reflects the August consumer spending surge: people charging more to credit cards and taking short-term loans. Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive, which tends to slow money creation, but that effect takes 6–12 months to show up fully. At 5.7%, M2 remains well above the 3–4% range the Fed would need to see for inflation to reliably return to 2%. For households, rising M2 generally means continued price pressure on everyday goods and services — the monetary brake the Fed is applying needs time to slow this engine down.
Inflation
Core PCE Price Index (YoY)
3.0%
BREAKING LOWER ↓
August 2026 Core PCE — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — fell to 3.0% year-over-year (BEA, Personal Income and Outlays, released September 30), a 30-basis-point decline from two consecutive months at 3.3% and below every major estimate. The downside surprise was compounded by BEA's annual benchmark methodology revision (covering software, legal services, and portfolio fee calculation), which simultaneously lowered July Core PCE from 3.3% to 3.0% — meaning the Fed's preferred gauge has been at 3.0% for two consecutive months under the revised methodology. Headline PCE rose 3.4% YoY in August (with July revised from 3.7% to 3.4% under the same benchmark changes). The 30-basis-point decline in Core PCE was the single most market-moving data point of the week, driving CME FedWatch's October 28 hike probability from 76% to 18% within hours of the release. The 90-basis-point gap between Core CPI (2.4%) and Core PCE has narrowed to 60 basis points — the two measures are converging, which removes a key argument that PCE was structurally elevated beyond what CPI data suggested. At 3.0%, Core PCE is now 100 basis points above the Fed's 2% target, down from 130 basis points. Combined with the September jobs miss (+29,000 payrolls, unemployment 4.2%), the 3.0% Core PCE reading shifts the balance firmly toward a hold at the October 27–28 FOMC. The September Core PCE (due approximately October 30, BEA) will indicate whether the improvement is sustained.
In plain termsCore PCE is the Fed's preferred inflation measure — and it dropped to 3.0% in August, a bigger improvement than expected. The BEA also revised July's reading down from 3.3% to 3.0%, meaning inflation has been running cooler than previously known. At 3.0%, Core PCE is still 100 basis points above the Fed's 2% target — prices are still rising 50% faster than the Fed wants. But the direction has changed decisively: this is the first meaningful drop in Core PCE after two flat months at 3.3%, and it was the primary reason October 28 hike odds collapsed from 76% to 18% within a single day. For consumers: every $50 in spending is going up by about $1.50 per year at 3.0% — still uncomfortable, but down from $1.65 at 3.3%. The 2% target would mean only $1 per $50. The Fed's preferred gauge is moving in the right direction; if it continues to fall, borrowing costs (mortgages, credit cards, car loans) could begin to ease in 2027. The next reading arrives around October 30.

Nine consecutive expansion months is notable: manufacturing has absorbed the full Fed tightening cycle without a single contraction. The employment sub-index fall to 50.8 is directionally consistent with the September jobs report's 29,000 payroll miss and 4.2% unemployment rate — both pointing to a labor market that is decelerating without collapsing. With FOMC hold odds at 80% following the August Core PCE drop to 3.0% and the September jobs miss, the manufacturing sector's steady PMI provides a soft-landing argument. October ISM PMI (due November 3) will be the first post-October-28 FOMC read on manufacturing momentum.
In plain termsAny ISM reading above 50 means manufacturing is expanding; below 50 means it's contracting. At 54.5, U.S. factories are in solid growth for the ninth consecutive month — still-healthy new orders (53.2), positive employment (50.8), and strong production (57.1). The concern is Prices Paid, which surged to 77.9 — the highest since early 2022. When manufacturers pay more for inputs, prices eventually pass through to consumers. This is a warning signal that some of the inflation improvement seen in Core PCE (down to 3.0%) could face renewed pressure from factory costs in late 2026 and early 2027. For workers in manufacturing: nine consecutive expansion months means continued job security, but the employment sub-index is softening toward neutral. For rate watchers: the Prices Paid surge makes a rate cut in 2027 harder to justify, even as the weak jobs report and lower PCE argue for a hold at October 28.

Initial jobless claims for the week ending September 26 came in at 197,000 (DOL, released October 1), keeping the 4-week moving average at approximately 200,000 — near 57-year lows. The claims data arrived 24 hours before the September jobs report (+29,000 payrolls, unemployment 4.2%), creating a notable contrast: weekly layoff signals remain at historic lows even as total net hiring collapsed to a 14-month low. The interpretation is significant: employers are not actively laying workers off, but new hiring has slowed sharply — a late-cycle divergence characteristic of decelerating labor markets where momentum fades from the top (hiring) rather than the bottom (layoffs). ISM’s employment sub-index corroborates this reading: 50.8 in September, still expansionary but the softest since February. Historically, the combination of ultra-low claims and weak payrolls tends to precede a more substantive rise in the unemployment rate 3–6 months forward — meaning the 4.2% September figure may not be the peak. For the FOMC: low claims reduce urgency for a cut (the labor market is not in distress), while the payroll miss reduces urgency for a hike — together they underpin the 80% hold probability at October 27–28. The next claims report (for week ending October 3, released October 9) provides the first Q4 labor market read.
In plain termsFor the week ending September 26, 197,000 people filed for unemployment benefits — near 57-year lows, meaning very few workers are being laid off. The 4-week average is 200,000. But this stands in striking contrast with the September jobs report released the very next day: employers only added 29,000 new jobs — far below the roughly 150,000 needed to keep pace with population growth. The paradox is important: companies aren’t firing people, but they have sharply cut back on hiring. For workers, this means good job security if you have a job, but harder to find a new one. For the economy: this pattern — low layoffs but weak hiring — often appears in the months before unemployment starts rising more noticeably. The 4.2% unemployment rate may not be the peak. For the Fed: low claims argue against an emergency cut; weak payrolls argue against a hike. The result is a hold at 80% odds on October 28.

Editor's Analysis

Three Days Changed Everything: Core PCE Breaks to 3.0%, September Jobs Miss by 100,000, and the Fed's October Hike Goes from 76% Certainty to Long Shot

Core PCE 3.0% (BEA Aug); NFP +29K, UR 4.2% (BLS Sep); 10Y 5.28% (highest since 2002); FOMC October hold 80%; 30-yr mortgage 7.28% — Written by AI agents designed and managed by Connor Leary, October 4, 2026

On September 30, the BEA released August Personal Income and Outlays data showing Core PCE at 3.0% year-over-year — down 30 basis points from two consecutive months at 3.3% and below every major forecast. The downside surprise was compounded by BEA's annual benchmark methodology revision (covering software, legal services, and portfolio fee calculation), which retroactively lowered July Core PCE from 3.3% to 3.0% as well. Under the revised methodology, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge had been at 3.0% for two months without markets fully pricing it. The market response was immediate and decisive: CME FedWatch's October 28 hike probability fell from 76% to 18% within hours of the 8:30am ET release — the single largest single-day collapse in hike odds since the tightening cycle began. Headline PCE for August (including food and energy) also benefited from the same benchmark revision, landing at 3.4%. The 60-basis-point gap between Core CPI (2.4%) and Core PCE (3.0%) has narrowed meaningfully, removing a key market concern that the two measures were permanently diverging.

Two days later, on October 2, the Bureau of Labor Statistics delivered the September Employment Situation: +29,000 nonfarm payrolls — the weakest monthly print in 14 months and far below the roughly 120,000 needed to absorb labor force entrants. The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2%, from 4.1% in August. Prior months were revised lower by a combined -60,000 jobs: July was taken from +28,000 to -10,000 (an outright monthly contraction), and August was revised from +142,000 to +133,000. The -60,000 in downward revisions means the economy created fewer jobs in July and August than previously reported, making September's 29,000 even more sobering in cumulative context. The combination of a downside Core PCE surprise and a labor market deceleration arriving within 48 hours of each other is extraordinarily rare in macro calendar history — and the two together provided something neither could offer alone: a simultaneous case to hold on both the inflation mandate and the employment mandate.

"The September 30 Core PCE drop — under a methodology revision that retroactively confirmed two months of cooling — was the most consequential single data release of the entire tightening cycle."

The labor market paradox of this edition is worth stating precisely: initial jobless claims for the week ending September 26 came in at 197,000 (DOL, released October 1) — near 57-year lows — and yet September payrolls produced a 14-month low in job creation. Employers are not laying off workers, but they have sharply curtailed new hiring. This divergence — low layoffs, weak payrolls — is the characteristic fingerprint of late-cycle labor market deceleration: the plane hasn't started descending yet, but the engines have throttled back. ISM's September employment sub-index corroborates at 50.8 — still above 50 (still expansionary) but the softest since February. Historically, the combination of ultra-low claims and weak payrolls tends to precede a more substantive rise in the unemployment rate 3–6 months forward. The 4.2% September unemployment rate may not be the peak.

The week's most overlooked data point was the ISM September Prices Paid sub-index: 77.9, the highest since early 2022 and a 6.6-point surge from August's 71.3. A Prices Paid reading above 70 historically predicts consumer goods inflation 3–6 months forward — a dynamic that could re-widen the Core PCE gap in Q1 2027 even as August and September show improvement. This is the single most important asterisk on the current disinflation thesis: upstream cost pressures are building in the manufacturing sector even as downstream consumer prices are easing. The pipeline typically transmits over 3–6 months, placing the risk squarely in Q1 2027. For Chair Warsh, the Prices Paid surge is the argument against premature celebration on inflation — and a key reason the dot plot may not fully abandon the 4.10% year-end median even if October 28 produces a hold.

With hold odds at 80% and hike odds at 18%, the October 27–28 FOMC has transformed from a near-foregone tightening conclusion into the most genuinely uncertain meeting of the cycle. Chair Warsh faces a significant communications challenge: the dot plot released after the September 16 hike called for a 4.10% year-end median rate — implicitly endorsing another hike. Now, with Core PCE having fallen 30 basis points in a single month under a revised methodology, the unemployment rate rising to 4.2%, and payrolls running at 29,000, an October hike would require the FOMC to explicitly override its own preferred inflation gauge's documented progress. Holding implicitly signals that 3.75–4.00% is the terminal rate for this cycle — a major policy communication that will move rate markets for months. The dot plot update is likely to be the most consequential output of the meeting: does the 2026 year-end median fall from 4.10% to 3.875% (signaling a hold and done), or does it remain at 4.10% (signaling December optionality)? One number will answer more questions than anything Warsh says at the press conference.

For households, the 30-year fixed mortgage rose to 7.28% as of October 1 (Freddie Mac PMMS) — the highest of 2026 — even as hike odds collapsed. This apparently counterintuitive move reflects the long end of the Treasury curve rising on real-rate repricing: at 5.28%, the 10-year (its highest close since October 2002) is pricing a persistent above-target inflation environment independent of additional Fed tightening. A $400,000 home purchase now costs approximately $2,730 per month in principal and interest. If the FOMC holds on October 28 and signals that 3.75–4.00% is the terminal rate, the 10-year could correct toward 5.0–5.1% and 30-year mortgages toward 7.0–7.1% in the relief rally. The October 14 CPI and October 30 September Core PCE (one day after the FOMC decision) will be the next major inflation inflection points — and the ones that will confirm or undermine the narrative that September 30, 2026 marked the week inflation in America finally broke decisively lower.
