If you have a job, you're still relatively secure — layoffs remain near historically low levels. Consumer spending remained the primary growth driver, while government outlays were a drag. Two more revisions (second and final estimates) will update this figure before year-end. The downstream data released since the advance estimate has been uniformly softer: July nonfarm payrolls -23,000 (the first job loss in years) and July retail sales +5.0% YoY (down sharply from June's 6.7%) both point to Q3 growth tracking below the Q2 pace. ISM Manufacturing PMI remains a bright spot at 55.6 (four-year high), but factory output alone cannot sustain GDP growth if consumer demand contracts. Q3 2026 recession risk has increased meaningfully since the advance GDP estimate was released.
In plain termsThe economy grew at a +1.5% pace in Q2 2026 — still positive, but below Q1's +2.1%. This is a yellow flag: the economy is slowing, not collapsing. But data released since then has painted a more cautious picture: the July jobs report showed the first payroll loss in years, and now July retail sales show consumers spending less for the first time in months. A recession technically requires two consecutive quarters of negative growth, and Q2 is still positive. Q3 GDP won't be reported until late October — but the trajectory of the incoming data makes it the most-watched estimate since the tariff-driven Q1 2025 contraction.

Consumer Activity
Retail Sales Growth (YoY)
5.0%
MODERATING ↓
July 2026 advance retail sales came in at +5.0% year-over-year — down sharply from June's 6.7% and the weakest annual reading since early 2026. On a monthly basis, sales fell 0.6% from June to July, the first meaningful monthly decline in six months. The softness was broad-based: lower gasoline station receipts (reflecting another monthly drop in pump prices) contributed mechanically, but core measures also showed the payroll shock feeding through. Total sales for May through July are up 6.3% from a year ago — still above the ~4.75% long-run average, but the trajectory has shifted decisively. This is the first hard evidence that July's 23,000 payroll loss and prior-month downward revisions of 146,000 are translating into reduced consumer spending — the consumer had been the primary counterweight to labor market weakness through June.
In plain termsAmericans spent 0.6% less in July than in June — the first real pullback in months — and year-over-year retail growth slowed sharply from 6.7% to 5.0%. This is the first sign that the July payroll loss (-23,000 jobs) is hitting household budgets: when people lose jobs or fear losing them, they tend to spend less. The 5% YoY figure is still positive (above the ~4.75% long-run average), so this is a slowdown, not a collapse. But the direction matters for the Fed: a weakening consumer alongside easing inflation strengthens the case for a September hold and reduces the chance of a rate hike at the September 16–17 FOMC meeting.
Money Supply
M2 Money Supply Growth (YoY)
5.5%
ELEVATED →
M2 grew 5.5% year-over-year through June 2026, per the Federal Reserve's H.6 release published July 28 — a slight moderation from May's 5.6%. Total M2 now stands at $23.16 trillion. The July 2026 H.6 data is expected around late August and will provide the first read on whether July's payroll weakness and consumer pullback have begun to slow money supply growth. Despite the minor monthly easing, M2 remains at elevated levels that historically precede inflation by 12–18 months. The two-month CPI improvement (from 4.2% in May to 3.4% in July) is directionally positive, but the structural money supply overhang continues to provide a floor for inflationary pressure well into 2027. A durable return to 2% inflation likely requires M2 growth falling toward 3–4%.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money in circulation — cash, bank accounts, money market funds. At 5.5% growth, there is still more money in the economy than the real output justifies. This excess liquidity is why inflation — even as it eases from 4.2% to 3.4% — remains above 3%: there is simply too much money chasing goods and services. Historically, elevated M2 growth feeds into consumer prices with a 12–18 month lag, meaning the money pumped into the economy in late 2025 continues to exert upward pressure on prices now. The July H.6 (expected late August) will offer the next read on whether the consumer slowdown is translating into slower money supply growth.

The seventh consecutive month of expansion, with 15 of 18 industries reporting growth. Production jumped to 58.5 (five-year high); new orders rose to 56.7; the employment sub-index crossed above 50 for the first time in 33 months (52.8), signaling factory hiring expansion. The Prices Paid index eased to 71.1 (third straight monthly decline). The August ISM PMI will be released September 2 — markets will watch whether the manufacturing boom holds against the broader consumer slowdown. The July retail sales miss (+5.0% vs June's 6.7%) raises the question of whether goods demand can support continued factory expansion if household spending continues to decelerate. A sustained factory sector remains the clearest counterargument to recession risk.
In plain termsAny ISM reading above 50 means manufacturing is growing; below 50 means shrinking. At 55.6, U.S. factories are expanding at their fastest pace in four years, and manufacturers added workers in July for the first time in nearly three years. This is genuinely good news — manufacturing jobs tend to pay well, and strong factory output supports the broader economy. The friction: consumer spending is now pulling back (-0.6% monthly in July), raising questions about whether goods demand can sustain the factory expansion over the next few months. For the Fed, a booming factory sector gives the three hawkish dissenters an argument: the economy isn't breaking, so why not hike? August data (due Sep 2) will test whether the expansion continues.

Editor's Analysis

The Consumer Cracks: CPI Eases to 3.4% and Retail Sales Slow Sharply to 5.0% — September FOMC Now a True Coin Flip at 52/48

CPI July 3.4% (core 2.5%, lowest since '24), retail +5.0% YoY (-0.6% monthly), 10Y Treasury 4.70%, Sept FOMC 52% hold / 48% hike — By Connor Leary, August 16, 2026

Two weeks of data have completed the picture for September's FOMC. The July CPI, released August 12, registered 3.4% year-over-year — down from June's 3.5% — with core CPI falling to 2.5%, its lowest reading since late 2024. Energy prices declined 1.5% month-over-month in July, extending June's 5.7% monthly drop, while shelter costs eased further to 3.2% YoY. The monthly print was just +0.1% — a reading consistent with a genuine disinflationary trajectory. Two days later, the Census Bureau released July retail sales: -0.6% month-over-month, +5.0% year-over-year, down sharply from June's 6.7% and the weakest annual reading since early 2026. Together, these two reports provide the first hard evidence that the July payroll shock (-23,000 nonfarm jobs) is feeding through to household behavior. The CME FedWatch repriced modestly on each release, settling at approximately 52% hold / 48% hike for the September 16–17 meeting — essentially the most uncertain FOMC outcome in years.

The CPI details reward a closer look. Headline inflation has now fallen from 4.2% in May to 3.4% in July — an 80 basis-point improvement in two months that is the most sustained disinflationary run since the 2024 normalization period. Core CPI at 2.5% is within 50 basis points of the Fed's implied core target zone. Shelter, which had been the most stubborn component, continued its gradual retreat to 3.2% YoY. The ISM Prices Paid index has now declined for three consecutive months (71.1 in July), adding factory-floor confirmation that goods inflation is easing from the upstream. If the August 26 Core PCE reflects the same improvement telegraphed by the core CPI reading, the September debate will tilt meaningfully toward a hold.

"The disinflationary signal arrived — but so did the first real sign of consumer fatigue. September's decision now truly hangs in the balance."

The retail sales miss deserves equal weight. The 0.6% monthly decline is the first meaningful pullback in six months, and the YoY deceleration from 6.7% to 5.0% is large enough to raise genuine questions about Q3 consumer momentum. The consumer had been the clearest counterargument to recession risk through June — retail spending had absorbed both a weakening labor market and elevated borrowing costs with remarkable durability. That durability is now in question. The initial jobless claims reading for the week ending August 8 (weekly 209,000, 4-week average 199,000) continues to show that employers are not broadly laying off workers — the "no-hire, no-fire" paradox persists — but the combination of a payroll loss and a consumer pullback creates a feedback risk that the Fed cannot ignore.

The Treasury market's verdict on the week was a modest yield backup. The 10-year Treasury rose from its August 7 post-jobs low of 4.65% to 4.70% by August 14, as the in-line CPI print was interpreted as "not soft enough to close the September door." The 2-year yield settled at 4.18%, widening the 2s10s spread to +52 bps — still firmly normal-shaped. The 30-year yield rose to 5.26%, maintaining the long-end inflation premium. For mortgage borrowers, 30-year fixed rates remain in the 7.1–7.3% range, and the path to 7.0% depends entirely on whether the incoming data — August 26 Core PCE, September 4 jobs report — tilts the committee toward a hold.

The three weeks between now and September 16 are the highest-stakes data window of the current cycle. The August 26 Core PCE release is the last major inflation read before the decision; the September 4 jobs report will determine whether July's payroll loss was a one-off or the start of a deteriorating trend. Chair Warsh and the majority held in July on the back of a decelerating labor market; three dissenters wanted to hike on elevated inflation. Now, the data has moved on both fronts: inflation is easing, but so is consumer spending. The September meeting will require the committee to decide whether the 140 bps gap between CPI (3.4%) and target (2.0%) justifies another hike into a softening economy — or whether the July payroll loss and consumer pullback have already done enough tightening work for the Fed. Either decision could be defended. That's what makes September's FOMC the most consequential in years.
