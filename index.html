Monetary Policy
Federal Funds Rate
3.88%
HELD: DECEMBER HIKE ~79% ODDS ↑
The FOMC voted 10-2 on October 28 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.75–4.00% — a decision hawkishly validated by all data since. Governors Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented in favor of an immediate hike. The October 29 BEA releases confirmed their case: Q3 2026 GDP advanced +3.4% and Core PCE rose to 3.1% YoY. The October jobs report (BLS, released November 6) then removed any residual ambiguity: nonfarm payrolls jumped +165,000 — well above consensus estimates near 65,000 — while unemployment fell to 4.1% from 4.2%. September payrolls were revised up to +60,000 from the initial +29,000 reading. Average hourly earnings rose 3.1% YoY (+0.4% MoM), matching Core PCE — no compression of real wage growth, which removes a potential dovish argument. October ISM Manufacturing PMI reached 55.5 (released November 2) — the tenth consecutive expansion month. At 3.75–4.00%, the real policy rate (nominal minus Core PCE 3.1%) stands at approximately +67–83 basis points positive — restrictive but narrowing as inflation re-accelerates. With a 3.4%-growth economy, 4.1% unemployment, 55.5 ISM, and Core PCE at 3.1%, the Fed faces no credible argument to pause in December. CME FedWatch now prices December 9 at approximately 79% hike / 21% hold / 0% cut. The final pre-decision data point is October CPI on November 12 — if headline comes in above 3.3%, hike probability likely rises above 85%.
In plain termsThe Fed held rates steady on October 28 — but two members voted to hike immediately, and the data since has validated that impulse decisively. The October jobs report (November 6) added +165,000 payrolls and unemployment fell to 4.1%. October ISM Manufacturing expanded for a tenth straight month (55.5). CME FedWatch now prices December 9 at approximately 79% hike / 21% hold. For your wallet: the 30-year fixed mortgage eased to 7.18% (Freddie Mac PMMS, November 5) as the 10-year yield fell to 4.86% — a $400,000 loan costs roughly $2,710 per month, about $82 less than October’s 7.40% peak. But with December at 79% hike probability, that relief remains fragile: October CPI (November 12) is the last inflation reading before the decision. Credit card APRs above 22% and car loan rates near 8.5% will not ease until the Fed clearly signals it is finished — which the current data trajectory does not support before mid-2027.
Inflation ⚠ WATCH
Consumer Price Index (YoY)
3.7%
REACCELERATING: CONFIRMED BY CORE PCE ↑
September 2026 CPI rose 3.7% year-over-year (BLS, released October 14) — the highest headline print since June 2022. The September signal has now been confirmed by Core PCE rising to 3.1% YoY (BEA, released October 29) — up from 3.0% in August. The two-measure convergence is significant: Core CPI at 3.1% and Core PCE at 3.1% are now in lockstep, eliminating the divergence that some analysts used to argue the CPI data was overstating true inflation. The dominant drivers of September CPI remain energy (20.4% YoY; gasoline +34.2% YoY; +5.2% monthly), shelter (4.2% YoY, surged from 3.0% in August), and the Core CPI component running above 3.0% for the first time since early 2025. October 2026 CPI — the last inflation print before December 9 — is due November 12. The 170-basis-point gap between headline CPI (3.7%) and the Fed’s 2% target, now confirmed by the same gap in Core PCE (3.1%), has effectively ended the debate about December: CME FedWatch places a hike at approximately 75% probability. ISM Services Prices Paid at 74.0 (the highest since July 2022) and Manufacturing Prices Paid at 77.9 signal continued pipeline cost pressure through Q4.
In plain termsPrices rose 3.7% in September compared to a year ago. The main culprit: gasoline surged 9.1% in a single month and is up 34.2% from a year ago. Strip out volatile food and energy and Core CPI jumped to 3.1% — and that same 3.1% reading was confirmed by Core PCE (the Fed’s preferred measure) on October 29. Shelter costs surged to 4.2% over the year — a sharp re-acceleration from 3.0% in August, hitting renters and would-be homebuyers hard. The October jobs report (+165,000 payrolls, 4.1% unemployment, released November 6) has locked in December 9 as a near-certain hike meeting — now 79% on FedWatch. The sole wildcard: October CPI on November 12. If it comes in above 3.5%, December hike probability rises above 85%. A surprise below 3.0% (possible if energy moderates) could reopen the debate, but that remains the minority scenario given Core CPI’s 3.1% momentum.

The Q3 surge from Q2’s final +2.2% to +3.4% has since been validated by October’s labor market: nonfarm payrolls jumped +165,000 (November 6) and unemployment fell to 4.1%, consistent with an economy that absorbed the Fed’s tightening without meaningful deceleration. For the Fed, a +3.4% print combined with October payrolls at +165,000 and ISM Manufacturing at 55.5 (October) is an overwhelming argument against December pause. The second estimate is due November 25, 2026 — historical patterns suggest the advance estimate moves about 0.3–0.5 percentage points by the final reading. With consumer spending strong and manufacturing in a tenth consecutive expansion month, revision risk appears symmetric to slightly upward.

Consumer spending, investment, and government outlays all contributed. The October jobs report (+165,000 payrolls, 4.1% unemployment) has since confirmed that Q3’s strength carried into Q4: the economy is not slowing. That combination — +3.4% GDP plus strong October hiring — leaves the Fed with no growth-based reason to hold in December. CME FedWatch prices December 9 at approximately 79% hike. For workers: strong GDP plus strong October payrolls mean job security and stable wages. Consumer Activity
Retail Sales Growth (YoY)
6.3%
STRONG BUT ENERGY-DRIVEN ↑
September 2026 retail and food services sales rose 0.8% from August to $745.0 billion, and 6.3% year-over-year (Census Bureau, released October 15) — a headline beat against the 0.3%–0.6% monthly consensus. August was revised upward to +1.3% MoM (from +1.2%). The headline strength was almost entirely gasoline-driven: sales at gasoline stations surged 6.4% on the month, reflecting the 9.1% gas price spike in the September CPI. The control group — which strips out autos, gasoline, building materials, and food services and directly inputs to GDP — rose just 0.1% for the month. Excluding autos and gasoline, the gain was an equally thin 0.2%. The YoY rate of +6.3% (up from +6.0%) reflects the same energy distortion that produced the hot CPI headline. The August upward revision to +1.3% is constructive for Q3 GDP tracking. For the Fed, the nominal headline beat confirms consumer activity remains intact — but the gasoline-driven composition signals inflation passthrough rather than organic demand strength. September retail's energy distortion could reverse sharply if oil prices moderate in October. The next report (October 2026 data) is scheduled for November 17.
In plain termsAmericans spent more in September — retail sales beat expectations at +0.8% for the month and +6.3% over the prior year. But look under the hood: gasoline stations accounted for almost all of the monthly gain (+6.4% in September), reflecting higher gas prices — not more economic activity. Strip out gas and the consumer picture is much flatter, with the control group up just 0.1%. For everyday households: you're spending more at the pump, which inflates the overall sales total. Higher gas prices also feed back into CPI (3.7% in September), which potentially keeps the Fed in tightening mode longer. The silver lining: August was revised up to +1.3%, which helps Q3 GDP tracking. If oil prices moderate in October, both retail sales and CPI could look considerably softer in the November reports — a potential turning point.

The September jobs report’s weakness (+29,000 payrolls) had provided a temporary counterargument, but Q3 GDP’s +3.4% advance estimate (October 29) demonstrates the broader economy is absorbing elevated monetary conditions without a growth shortfall. Historical analysis suggests M2 growth above 5.5% is structurally incompatible with 2% CPI over any sustained 12-month horizon. The October H.6 release is due approximately November 24, 2026.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money circulating in the economy — cash, bank accounts, money market funds. At 5.7% annual growth in September (confirmed October 27), the money supply is growing faster than the Fed wants. Two consecutive months at 5.7% tell the same story: too much money chasing too few goods. At 5.7% M2 growth alongside 3.7% CPI and 3.1% Core PCE, the Fed’s mathematical case for further tightening is intact. For households: this monetary backdrop means continued price pressure on everyday goods and services — the Fed’s brake is not yet slowing the economy enough. Higher interest rates constrain borrowing and reduce money creation, but that effect takes 6–12 months to show fully. The next M2 reading arrives around November 24.

November ISM Manufacturing PMI is due the first business day of December 2026.
In plain termsAny ISM reading above 50 means manufacturing is expanding; below 50 means contracting. At 55.5, U.S. factories are in solid growth for the tenth straight month — a streak that now spans the Fed’s entire 2026 tightening cycle. Production (59.0) is the highest since November 2021. Employment expanded for a fourth consecutive month (52.4). Prices Paid at 75.2: while down from September’s 77.9, this is the ninth consecutive month above 70 — manufacturers are still paying significantly elevated input costs that eventually flow to consumers. October Payrolls Clear the Last Hurdle: +165K Jobs, 4.1% Unemployment, ISM at 55.5 Lock December 9 at 79% Hike — November 12 CPI Is the Final Witness

10Y Treasury 4.86% (post-jobs easing); Oct NFP +165K (Sep revised +45K); unemployment 4.1%; ISM Oct 55.5 (Prices Paid 75.2); claims 189K / 195K 4-wk avg; Dec 9 hike ~79% — Written by AI agents designed and managed by Connor Leary, November 8, 2026

The October jobs report (released November 6) answered the last serious question about December 9. Nonfarm payrolls rose +165,000 — a strong rebound from September’s initially reported +29,000, which itself was revised up to +45,000. The revision matters: September was not the beginning of a deceleration, it was an anomaly in a tightening labor market. Unemployment fell to 4.1% from 4.2%. For perspective: this report arrived against a backdrop of 3.75–4.00% interest rates, 10-year yields near 5%, and a 30-year mortgage at 7.18%. The economy is not reacting to tight monetary policy the way textbooks predict. It is absorbing it — and hiring more people. That is the scenario that leaves Chair Warsh no credible case for holding in December.

Three days earlier, the ISM Manufacturing PMI for October confirmed the expansion story from the production side. ISM came in at 55.5 — up 1.0 point from September’s 54.5, the tenth consecutive month above 50 (the expansion threshold). New orders surged to 57.4 from 53.2, and production jumped to 59.0 from 57.1. Employment rose to 51.3 from 50.8. Critically: Prices Paid registered 75.2 — down from September’s 77.9 but still the second-highest reading since early 2022. For context, any Prices Paid reading above 70 signals material pipeline cost pressure. Two consecutive months above 75 means the September CPI surge to 3.7% is not being followed by rapid disinflation. October CPI (November 12) will tell us whether October manufacturing costs have already fed through.

”October’s +165,000 payrolls don’t just answer September’s 29,000 miss — they prove the miss was an outlier in a 3.4%-growth economy that the Fed has no case to let run unchecked into 2027.”

The bond market’s reaction to the jobs report was counterintuitive but instructive. The 10-year yield settled at 4.86% on November 6 — down 8 basis points from October 30’s 4.94%, despite the strong payrolls beat. The 2-year fell to 4.51% (from 4.56%), and the 2s10s spread narrowed to +35 bps (from +38 bps). The curve interpretation: markets had already priced most of the December hike after October 29’s GDP/PCE data. The jobs report confirmed the story rather than changing it. More tellingly, the 30-year bond eased to 5.26% (from 5.31%) — suggesting that the terminal rate debate, the question of how high the Fed ultimately goes, is not being revised aggressively higher. CME FedWatch moved December 9 to approximately 79% hike / 21% hold. The revision from 75% to 79% is incremental; the market was already committed to December.

Jobless claims for the week ending November 1 (released November 6) printed at 189,000 — the lowest weekly reading since July 2026 — confirming the tight labor market picture. The 4-week moving average stands at approximately 195,000. These ultra-low claims numbers are not new: the fourth consecutive week near or below 190–195K underscores that while October’s hiring pace was strong, the more consistent story is that layoffs are not happening. The labor market paradox of this cycle — slow hiring alongside almost no layoffs — has resolved in the strong-hiring direction. The NY Fed 12-month recession probability model stands at 13.9%, consistent with a commonly used rule of thumb for mild recession risk; note that the NBER uses a broader set of monthly indicators and does not rely solely on consecutive quarterly GDP data.

The focus narrows entirely to November 12 CPI. The October Core PCE (November 25) arrives after the December 9 decision — the committee will not see it before it votes. October CPI is the final inflation checkpoint. With October payrolls confirming labor market strength, ISM Prices Paid at 75.2 signaling pipeline pressure, and December odds at 79%, the data bar for a December hold has risen sharply. Only a severe October CPI undershoot — core below 2.5% — could plausibly reopen the debate. For households: the Freddie Mac 30-year mortgage rate is 7.18% (PMMS November 6). A $400,000 loan costs approximately $2,710 per month. A December hike to 4.00–4.25% would likely push the 10-year back toward 4.90–5.00%, with mortgages following to 7.30–7.40% — adding $50–80 per month. That is the household cost of an economy that refuses to slow down.
