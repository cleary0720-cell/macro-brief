Monetary Policy
Federal Funds Rate
3.88%
HELD: DECEMBER HIKE ~75% ODDS ↑
The FOMC voted 10-2 on October 28 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.75–4.00% — but the decision masked a hawkish undercurrent. Governors Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both dissented, voting to raise rates immediately to 4.00–4.25% — the first dissents in favor of tightening this cycle. The statement upgraded growth language from “solid” to “expanding at a strong pace.” Chair Warsh signaled December 9 remains a live meeting. The post-meeting data validated the hawks’ case: Q3 2026 GDP came in at +3.4% annualized (advance estimate, BEA, October 29) — the strongest quarterly growth in over a year — while Core PCE rose to 3.1% YoY (BEA, Personal Income and Outlays, released October 29), the first increase since April. CME FedWatch places December 9 at approximately 75% hike / 25% hold / 0% cut as of late October. At 3.75–4.00%, the real policy rate (nominal minus Core PCE 3.1%) is approximately +67–83 bps positive — still restrictive but the inflation re-acceleration has closed the real-rate cushion. The October jobs report (November 6) and October CPI (November 12) are the final major data points before the December 9 decision. With two dissenters already voted to hike, even a modest beat in either release could lock in December.
In plain termsThe Fed held rates steady on October 28 — but two members voted to hike immediately, a rare hawkish signal. The very next day brought Q3 GDP at +3.4% (much stronger than expected) and September Core PCE at 3.1% (inflation re-accelerated). Those two data points have locked in December 9 as a near-certain hike meeting, now priced at roughly 75% on FedWatch. For your wallet: the 30-year fixed mortgage eased to approximately 7.20% as the 10-year yield fell to 4.94% post-FOMC — a $400,000 loan now costs roughly $2,695 per month, about $50 less than October’s 7.40% peak. But with December at 75% hike probability, credit card APRs above 22% and car loan rates near 8.5% will not ease meaningfully until the Fed signals it is finished — which current data does not support before mid-2027.
Inflation ⚠ WATCH
Consumer Price Index (YoY)
3.7%
REACCELERATING: CONFIRMED BY CORE PCE ↑
September 2026 CPI rose 3.7% year-over-year (BLS, released October 14) — the highest headline print since June 2022. The September signal has now been confirmed by Core PCE rising to 3.1% YoY (BEA, released October 29) — up from 3.0% in August. The two-measure convergence is significant: Core CPI at 3.1% and Core PCE at 3.1% are now in lockstep, eliminating the divergence that some analysts used to argue the CPI data was overstating true inflation. The dominant drivers of September CPI remain energy (20.4% YoY; gasoline +34.2% YoY; +5.2% monthly), shelter (4.2% YoY, surged from 3.0% in August), and the Core CPI component running above 3.0% for the first time since early 2025. October 2026 CPI — the last inflation print before December 9 — is due November 12. The 170-basis-point gap between headline CPI (3.7%) and the Fed’s 2% target, now confirmed by the same gap in Core PCE (3.1%), has effectively ended the debate about December: CME FedWatch places a hike at approximately 75% probability. ISM Services Prices Paid at 74.0 (the highest since July 2022) and Manufacturing Prices Paid at 77.9 signal continued pipeline cost pressure through Q4.
In plain termsPrices rose 3.7% in September compared to a year ago. The main culprit: gasoline surged 9.1% in a single month and is up 34.2% from a year ago. Strip out volatile food and energy and Core CPI jumped to 3.1% — and that same 3.1% reading was confirmed by Core PCE (the Fed’s preferred measure) on October 29. Shelter costs surged to 4.2% over the year — a sharp re-acceleration from 3.0% in August, hitting renters and would-be homebuyers hard. The key upcoming event: October CPI on November 12 — the last inflation reading before the December 9 FOMC decision. If October CPI comes in above 3.5%, a December hike (already 75% priced) becomes near-certain. If it drops below 3.0%, the December decision reopens. This single data point on November 12 may be the most consequential economic release of Q4 2026.
Labor Market
Unemployment Rate
4.2%
SLOWING: +29K SEP PAYROLLS ↓
The September 2026 employment situation (BLS, released October 2) delivered a significant downside surprise: nonfarm payrolls increased just 29,000 — far below the 84,000 consensus — while the unemployment rate rose to 4.2% from 4.1%. The report was compounded by substantial revisions: July was revised from +21,000 to -10,000 (down 31,000) and August was revised from +162,000 to +133,000 (down 29,000), subtracting a combined 60,000 jobs from previously reported totals. The three-month average for July through September is now +50,667 — well below the 2025 pace and consistent with a decelerating labor market. Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1% MoM and 3.0% YoY — the slowest annual wage growth of 2026 and below current CPI inflation (3.7%), meaning real wages turned negative. The paradox of ultra-low layoffs (jobless claims at 194K weekly, 197K 4-week avg) alongside weak hiring persists — a late-cycle pattern consistent with a slow-hiring, no-firing labor market. The FOMC held rates on October 28 (10-2 vote), partly due to this weak jobs backdrop. Now, with Q3 GDP at +3.4% and Core PCE at 3.1% pointing toward a December hike, the October jobs report (November 6) carries heightened importance: a payroll beat could remove the last impediment to December tightening; a second consecutive miss would reopen the December debate.
In plain termsSeptember added only 29,000 new jobs (vs. 84,000 forecast), unemployment rose to 4.2%, and prior months were revised down a combined 60,000 — July is now officially a monthly loss (-10,000). For workers: very few layoffs (claims at 194K weekly), so job security remains intact. But hiring has slowed sharply, and wage growth at 3.0% is trailing inflation at 3.7% — paychecks buy less in real terms. The key upcoming event: October jobs report on November 6. After Q3 GDP surprised at +3.4%, a strong payroll number would validate the soft-landing narrative and solidify December as a near-certain hike. A second weak print would complicate the Fed’s messaging around December.
Economic Output
GDP Growth Rate (Q3 2026, Advance)
+3.4%
STRONG REBOUND: BEST QUARTER IN A YEAR ↑
The BEA’s advance estimate for Q3 2026 GDP (released October 29) put annualized real growth at +3.4% — significantly beating the 1.5–2.0% consensus and the strongest quarterly reading in over a year. Growth was led by investment, consumer spending, and government outlays, with a decline in exports and an increase in imports providing partial offsets. The surprise upside is substantial: just one week before, the Q2 2026 final reading was +1.5% (later revised upward to 2.2% in BEA’s third estimate released September 30). The Q3 surge from 2.2% to 3.4% reverses the concerns raised by the weak September jobs report (+29,000 payrolls) and signals that the consumer and business investment channels remained robust through summer. For the Fed, a +3.4% print is a significant complication: the economy is not slowing under the weight of 3.75–4.00% interest rates. Combined with Core PCE rising to 3.1% on the same day, the GDP advance estimate all but confirmed December 9 as a live hike meeting. The second estimate is due November 25, 2026. Historical patterns suggest the advance estimate moves on average about 0.3–0.5 percentage points by the final reading — but with consumer spending strong and ISM Manufacturing running at 54.5, revision risk appears symmetric.
In plain termsThe U.S. economy grew at a +3.4% annualized rate in Q3 2026 — much stronger than the +1.5–2.0% economists expected. This is the best quarter in over a year. Investment, consumer spending, and government outlays all contributed. The concern is that this strong growth makes it harder for the Fed to justify pausing its rate hikes: the economy doesn’t need stimulus, and inflation (Core PCE at 3.1%) is re-accelerating. Two consecutive strong data points — Q3 GDP and Core PCE, both released on October 29 — have pushed December 9 hike odds to approximately 75%. For workers: strong GDP generally means job security and potential wage growth. For borrowers: it also means the Fed is more likely to keep raising rates, which keeps mortgages, car loans, and credit cards expensive into 2027.

The post-FOMC bond rally was modest: markets had already priced the hold, and the immediate post-decision rally was partially offset by the October 29 data — Q3 GDP at +3.4% and Core PCE at 3.1% — which put upward pressure on yields before the week closed. The 2s10s spread widened slightly to +38 bps (from +31 bps) as the short end rallied more aggressively on FOMC hold relief than the long end. The 3m10y spread widened to +85 bps (from +78 bps). Both remain positive, confirming a fully normal, upward-sloping curve. At 4.94%, the real 10-year yield (nominal minus Core PCE 3.1%) stands at approximately +184 basis points — meaningfully restrictive. The next major catalyst is October CPI (November 12), the last inflation print before December 9.
In plain termsThe 10-year Treasury yield eased to 4.94% after the FOMC held rates steady on October 28 — down from 5.00% earlier in the week and far below the October 7 peak of 5.36%. At 4.94%, an estimated 30-year fixed mortgage is approximately 7.20% — a $400,000 loan costs about $2,695 per month, roughly $50 less than October’s peak. For savers: 2-year Treasury yields at 4.56% still offer a real return above Core PCE (3.1%). The yield curve slopes normally from 1-month T-bills (4.06%) up to the 30-year (5.31%) — no recession signal from the bond market. But with December 9 at 75% hike probability, yields could rise again: a hot October CPI (November 12) would push the 10-year back toward 5.10–5.20% and mortgages back above 7.40%.
Consumer Activity
Retail Sales Growth (YoY)
6.3%
STRONG BUT ENERGY-DRIVEN ↑
September 2026 retail and food services sales rose 0.8% from August to $745.0 billion, and 6.3% year-over-year (Census Bureau, released October 15) — a headline beat against the 0.3%–0.6% monthly consensus. August was revised upward to +1.3% MoM (from +1.2%). The headline strength was almost entirely gasoline-driven: sales at gasoline stations surged 6.4% on the month, reflecting the 9.1% gas price spike in the September CPI. The control group — which strips out autos, gasoline, building materials, and food services and directly inputs to GDP — rose just 0.1% for the month. Excluding autos and gasoline, the gain was an equally thin 0.2%. The YoY rate of +6.3% (up from +6.0%) reflects the same energy distortion that produced the hot CPI headline. The August upward revision to +1.3% is constructive for Q3 GDP tracking. For the Fed, the nominal headline beat confirms consumer activity remains intact — but the gasoline-driven composition signals inflation passthrough rather than organic demand strength. September retail's energy distortion could reverse sharply if oil prices moderate in October. The next report (October 2026 data) is scheduled for November 17.
In plain termsAmericans spent more in September — retail sales beat expectations at +0.8% for the month and +6.3% over the prior year. But look under the hood: gasoline stations accounted for almost all of the monthly gain (+6.4% in September), reflecting higher gas prices — not more economic activity. Strip out gas and the consumer picture is much flatter, with the control group up just 0.1%. For everyday households: you're spending more at the pump, which inflates the overall sales total. Higher gas prices also feed back into CPI (3.7% in September), which potentially keeps the Fed in tightening mode longer. The silver lining: August was revised up to +1.3%, which helps Q3 GDP tracking. If oil prices moderate in October, both retail sales and CPI could look considerably softer in the November reports — a potential turning point.
Money Supply
M2 Money Supply Growth (YoY)
5.7%
PERSISTENTLY ELEVATED ↑
M2 grew 5.7% year-over-year in September 2026 — confirmed by the Federal Reserve’s H.6 release published October 27. This marks the second consecutive month at 5.7%, confirming the August re-acceleration was not a one-month anomaly. Total M2 reached $23.41 trillion (seasonally adjusted), up from $23.30 trillion in August. At 5.7%, M2 is materially above the 3–4% range the Fed would need to see for inflation to reliably return to 2%. The persistence of 5.7% M2 growth alongside September CPI at 3.7% and Core PCE re-accelerating to 3.1% presents the Fed with a classic monetary-quantity confirmation of the tightening mandate. The September jobs report’s weakness (+29,000 payrolls) had provided a temporary counterargument, but Q3 GDP’s +3.4% advance estimate (October 29) demonstrates the broader economy is absorbing elevated monetary conditions without a growth shortfall. Historical analysis suggests M2 growth above 5.5% is structurally incompatible with 2% CPI over any sustained 12-month horizon. The October H.6 release is due approximately November 24, 2026.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money circulating in the economy — cash, bank accounts, money market funds. At 5.7% annual growth in September (confirmed October 27), the money supply is growing faster than the Fed wants. Two consecutive months at 5.7% tell the same story: too much money chasing too few goods. At 5.7% M2 growth alongside 3.7% CPI and 3.1% Core PCE, the Fed’s mathematical case for further tightening is intact. For households: this monetary backdrop means continued price pressure on everyday goods and services — the Fed’s brake is not yet slowing the economy enough. Higher interest rates constrain borrowing and reduce money creation, but that effect takes 6–12 months to show fully. The next M2 reading arrives around November 24.

3.1%
RE-ACCELERATING: UP FROM 3.0% ↑
September 2026 Core PCE — the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge — rose to 3.1% year-over-year (BEA, Personal Income and Outlays, released October 29), up from 3.0% in August. The monthly gain was 0.3% (in line with expectations). Headline PCE rose to 3.6% YoY and 0.5% month-over-month, reflecting the energy price surge that drove September CPI. The Core PCE re-acceleration ends the two-month streak at 3.0% that had provided the dovish camp with its strongest argument. At 3.1%, Core PCE is now 110 basis points above the Fed’s 2% target — up from 100 bps in August. The convergence of Core CPI (3.1%) and Core PCE (3.1%) removes the longstanding measurement divergence that some analysts used to argue CPI was overstating true inflation. The October 29 PCE release, arriving the day after the FOMC held at 10-2, immediately validated the two dissenters (Cook and Hammack). CME FedWatch, which had priced December 9 at approximately 79–80% hike immediately post-FOMC, held at approximately 75% hike after the PCE print absorbed into market positioning. The next reading, October Core PCE (due November 25, BEA), arrives after the December 9 decision — making October CPI (November 12) the sole pre-decision inflation indicator for December.
In plain termsCore PCE — the Fed’s preferred inflation measure — rose to 3.1% in September, up from 3.0% in August. This matters enormously: it confirms that September’s hot CPI (3.7%) was not entirely driven by volatile gasoline prices — the underlying inflation trend re-accelerated too. At 3.1%, Core PCE is 110 basis points above the Fed’s 2% target — prices are rising 55% faster than the Fed wants. The re-acceleration, combined with Q3 GDP at +3.4%, all but ended the November-December debate: CME FedWatch has December 9 at ~75% hike probability. For consumers: every $50 in spending is going up by about $1.55 per year at 3.1% — compared to only $1.00 at the Fed’s 2% target. The next Core PCE update doesn’t arrive until November 25, after the December 9 decision, making October CPI (November 12) the last inflation checkpoint before the Fed must decide.

October ISM Manufacturing PMI (due November 3) will be the first post-FOMC-hold, post-GDP-surprise, post-Core-PCE-rebound read on whether the manufacturing expansion is sustained into Q4 2026 and whether Prices Paid — which has now been above 70 for three consecutive months — continues to signal forward inflation pressure.
In plain termsAny ISM reading above 50 means manufacturing is expanding; below 50 means contracting. At 54.5, U.S. factories are in solid growth for the ninth straight month — strong new orders (53.2), positive employment (50.8), and robust production (57.1). Q3 GDP at +3.4% is consistent with this expansion story. The concern is Prices Paid at 77.9 (the highest since 2022): when manufacturers pay more for inputs, those costs eventually flow to consumers. Three consecutive months above 70 is a sustained inflationary signal that aligns with CPI at 3.7% and Core PCE at 3.1%. October PMI arrives November 3 — if Prices Paid stays elevated above 70, the December 9 hike case gets even stronger. For manufacturing workers: nine consecutive expansion months mean continued job security and healthy activity levels.

Ultra-low claims alongside weak payrolls is a late-cycle signal historically resolved within 3–5 months into either a hiring recovery or a sudden layoff spike. The Most Consequential Week of Q4: FOMC Held 10-2, GDP Surged to +3.4%, Core PCE Re-Accelerated to 3.1% — December 9 Is Now the Fed’s Most Critical Decision in Years

10Y Treasury 4.94% (post-FOMC easing); FOMC held 10-2 (Cook & Hammack dissented); Q3 GDP +3.4% advance; Core PCE 3.1%; M2 Sep 5.7%; claims 194K / 197K 4-wk avg; Dec 9 hike ~75% — Written by AI agents designed and managed by Connor Leary, November 1, 2026

The week of October 27–31 delivered the most data-rich sequence of 2026 — and its message was unmistakable. The Federal Reserve held rates steady at 3.75–4.00% on October 28 (10-2 vote), as widely expected. But the decision carried a hawkish undercurrent that markets could not ignore: two committee members — Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack — dissented in favor of an immediate 25-basis-point hike. In the 18-meeting tightening cycle that began with the September 16 unanimous vote, two dissenters for more tightening is not a rounding error. It is a signal about where the committee’s center of gravity is heading. Chair Warsh’s post-meeting statement upgraded growth language from “solid” to “expanding at a strong pace,” and the press conference left December 9 conspicuously and deliberately open.

The day after the hold, the BEA released its advance estimate for Q3 2026 GDP: +3.4% annualized. Against a consensus centered on 1.5–2.0%, this was a substantial upside surprise and the best quarterly reading in over a year. Consumer spending, investment, and government outlays all contributed. More remarkable: this result arrived against a backdrop of 3.75–4.00% interest rates — the tightest monetary policy in two years. The economy absorbed the September hike and accelerated. That is the soft-landing scenario in its most optimistic form — but it also means the Fed has no growth argument for pausing December. You do not pause tightening when the economy is growing at 3.4%.

“Q3 GDP at +3.4%, Core PCE at 3.1%, two hawkish dissenters, and M2 running at 5.7% for two consecutive months — every data point from October 29 made December 9 more certain, not less.”

The same morning as the GDP print, the BEA released September Personal Income and Outlays. Core PCE rose to 3.1% YoY — up from 3.0% in August, the first increase since April, and exactly the number the two dissenters had implicitly predicted. The month-over-month gain was 0.3%, in line with expectations but incompatible with a 2% annual target. Headline PCE rose to 3.6% YoY. The Core PCE and Core CPI are now in lockstep at 3.1% — a convergence that removes the last remaining argument that PCE measurement divergence was masking true progress on inflation. The 110-basis-point gap between Core PCE (3.1%) and the Fed’s 2% target is now acknowledged on both measurement dimensions simultaneously. CME FedWatch absorbed the October 29 data and settled December 9 at approximately 75% hike probability. The logic is simple: the FOMC cannot credibly hold in December when growth is 3.4% and its preferred inflation gauge just re-accelerated.

Against this backdrop, the bond market’s response was measured. The 10-year Treasury eased from 5.00% (pre-FOMC) to 4.94% by October 30 — a modest 6-basis-point move that primarily reflected the hold relief rather than a fundamental reassessment of the December path. The short end moved further: the 2-year fell from 4.69% to 4.56% (-13 bps), steepening the 2s10s spread from +31 bps to +38 bps. The 30-year continued its climb to 5.31%, pricing in elevated term premium as December tightening approaches. M2 confirmed at 5.7% YoY in September (H.6 released October 27) — a second consecutive month at the same level, removing any hope that August was a transient re-acceleration. Jobless claims for the week ending October 24 (released October 30) held near 57-year lows at 194K weekly / 197K 4-week average. The labor market is not providing the Fed with a reason to pause.

The critical question for December 9 now rests with two remaining data releases: the October jobs report (November 6) and October CPI (November 12). The former will test whether September’s 29,000 payroll miss was an anomaly in a 3.4%-growth economy or the beginning of a deceleration. The latter is the last inflation reading the FOMC will see before December 9 — October Core PCE (November 25) arrives after the decision. For households: the 30-year mortgage has eased to approximately 7.20% as yields settled post-FOMC, a $400,000 loan saving roughly $50 per month versus October’s peak. But that relief is fragile. A hot October CPI on November 12 could push December hike odds above 85% and send the 10-year back toward 5.20% — with mortgages following to 7.50% before Thanksgiving. A cool print could reopen the December debate and allow continued yield easing. The data window narrows by the day.
