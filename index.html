Key Economic Indicators

Monetary Policy
Federal Funds Rate
3.63%
ON HOLD →
The FOMC voted unanimously 12-0 on June 17 to hold the target range at 3.50–3.75% — the fifth consecutive hold and the first full decision cycle under new Chair Kevin Warsh. The June dot plot remains hawkish: nine of 18 members project at least one hike before year-end. The July 29 meeting is now three days away — and in a dramatic intraweek reversal, hike odds have tripled from 10% to 38%. A renewed escalation of the U.S.-Iran conflict at the Strait of Hormuz sent oil prices back above $87 per barrel this week, reigniting the very inflation fear that the June CPI had quieted. One week ago, hike odds were just 10% — but a new Iran-Hormuz escalation sent oil prices sharply higher this week, and hike odds tripled to 38%. The base case is still a hold (62%), but this meeting is far more uncertain than it looked a week ago. If the Fed hikes on Tuesday, mortgage rates, car loans, and credit card rates will all rise immediately — a quarter-point hike means roughly $65–$75 more per month on a $400,000 mortgage. A hold changes nothing for borrowing costs, but Chair Warsh's tone at the press conference will signal where the Fed is headed next.
Inflation ⚠ WATCH
Consumer Price Index (YoY)
3.5%
EASING ↓
June 2026 CPI rose 3.5% year-over-year — the first meaningful decline since the Iran-driven energy surge began, falling sharply from May's 4.2% and beating the 3.9% consensus forecast. Energy prices fell 5.7% month-over-month in June (the largest single-month decline since April 2020), pulling the annual energy reading from 23.5% to 15.7%. Core CPI, excluding food and energy, eased from 2.9% to 2.6% — the lowest core reading since early 2025, and below the 2.8% forecast. Shelter costs decelerated to 3.3% YoY. The report effectively ended market speculation about a July 29 rate hike: CME FedWatch moved from 25% hike probability to 10%, with hold at 90%. The June data is the first genuine disinflationary signal of 2026.
In plain termsInflation fell meaningfully in June — from 4.2% to 3.5% — largely because gasoline and energy prices dropped sharply from Iran-related highs. Strip out energy, and core prices are up just 2.6%, the best reading in over a year. Groceries, rent, and services are still more expensive than a year ago, but the pace of increase is slowing. This June CPI report almost certainly means the Fed will not hike rates on July 29. Mortgage rates and borrowing costs are unlikely to rise in the near term, though they remain historically elevated.
Labor Market
Unemployment Rate
4.2%
MISLEADING DECLINE ↓
June payrolls added just 57,000 jobs — the weakest monthly print in years, barely half the consensus estimate of 115,000, and a dramatic reversal from May's 172,000. The unemployment rate technically fell from 4.3% to 4.2%, but the mechanism was troubling: labor force participation dropped 0.3 percentage points to 61.5%, the lowest since March 2021. Workers are exiting the labor force, not finding new jobs. Leisure and hospitality shed 61,000 positions in June, while professional and business services and social assistance provided the only meaningful offsets. Average hourly earnings rose 0.3% month-over-month ($37.64), consistent with the prior trend. The headline rate improved for the wrong reason.
In plain termsThe unemployment rate dropped to 4.2%, but the underlying story is weaker than it looks. Only 57,000 new jobs were created in June — roughly half of what economists expected — and the rate fell mainly because 300,000 people stopped looking for work entirely (the lowest participation rate since March 2021). If you're job-hunting right now, the labor market is getting meaningfully harder. The Fed is watching carefully, but markets still price only a 24% chance of a July cut.
Economic Output
GDP Growth Rate (Q1 2026, Final Est.)
+2.1%
EXPANDING ▲
The BEA's third and final estimate for Q1 2026 GDP, released June 25, revised growth up to +2.1% annualized — a full 0.5 percentage point above the +1.6% second estimate and above the original +2.0% advance reading. The upward revision was driven primarily by a downward adjustment to imports (which subtract from GDP), partially offset by a modest downward revision to consumer spending. Q1 2026 now stands as the strongest quarterly growth reading since Q2 2025's +2.8%, confirming that the economy entered 2026 on solid footing despite tariff headwinds and geopolitical uncertainty.
In plain termsGDP measures the total output of the US economy. The final Q1 2026 reading of +2.1% — revised UP from the initial +1.6% estimate — shows the economy was growing faster than initially thought at the start of the year. No recession signal here. Paradoxically, strong GDP growth may help the Fed cut rates: a healthy economy doesn't need emergency-level monetary restraint, and with the labor market showing early softening signals, the Fed has room to ease without triggering overheating.
Fixed Income
10-Year Treasury Yield
4.69%
RISING ↑
The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.69% as of July 24 — up 14 basis points from last week's 4.55%, the largest weekly move since the initial Iran shock in March. The entire yield curve shifted materially higher: the 2-year rose to 4.33% (+15 bps), the 5-year to 4.46% (+18 bps), and the 30-year to 5.16% (+7 bps). The driver is the same as last month's spike: a renewed Strait of Hormuz escalation sent oil prices toward $90 per barrel, pushing CME FedWatch hike odds from 10% to 38% within a week. The 2s10s spread narrowed to +36 basis points; the 3m10y spread widened to +79 bps. The curve remains normally shaped — no inversion — but the parallel shift higher is putting pressure on all rate-sensitive sectors. With the July 29 FOMC now three days away at 38% hike odds, yields are unlikely to settle until the decision is announced.
In plain termsThe 10-year Treasury yield sets the floor for mortgage rates. At 4.69% — up sharply from 4.55% last week — a 30-year fixed mortgage likely costs around 7.2–7.4% right now, meaning a $400,000 home loan runs approximately $2,710–$2,760 per month in principal and interest. The jump was driven by new Iran-Hormuz tensions and surging hike odds (now 38% for July 29). If the Fed actually hikes on Tuesday, yields could climb further. If it holds and signals patience, yields may pull back modestly. Either way, meaningful mortgage relief remains elusive in the near term.
Consumer Activity
Retail Sales Growth (YoY)
6.7%
EXPANDING ↑
June 2026 retail and food services sales reached $768.6 billion — up 6.7% year-over-year and 0.2% month-over-month, the fifth consecutive monthly gain. The softer monthly pace reflects lower gasoline station receipts (down 5.3% on cheaper pump prices), partially offset by solid gains in nonstore retailers and sporting goods. Total sales for April through June are up 6.4% from a year ago. The slight deceleration from May's 6.9% annual rate is not alarming — gasoline deflation mechanically pulls the number lower — and core retail sales (excluding autos and gas) remain robust. Released July 16, the data confirm that consumers continued spending through June's labor market softness.
In plain termsAmericans kept spending solidly in June — retail sales grew 6.7% year-over-year, the fifth straight monthly gain. The slight dip from May's 6.9% is largely explained by cheaper gas prices pulling down receipts at gas stations. Strip that out and consumer spending remains healthy. Even with a weak June jobs report (+57,000 payrolls) and elevated borrowing costs, consumers haven't pulled back — a good sign for economic growth, though it also gives the Fed less incentive to rush toward rate cuts.
Money Supply
M2 Money Supply Growth (YoY)
5.6%
ACCELERATING ↑
M2 expanded 5.6% year-over-year through May 2026, per the Federal Reserve's H.6 release published June 23 — up sharply from April's 4.7% and the fastest growth rate since the post-pandemic reflation era. Total money supply now stands at approximately $23.1 trillion. The near-one-percentage-point monthly acceleration is the week's most underappreciated development: historically, M2 expansion leads inflation by 12–18 months, meaning today's money supply surge could sustain elevated price levels well into 2027 even if energy-driven CPI moderates in the near term.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money circulating through the economy — cash, checking accounts, savings, and money market funds. The jump from 4.7% to 5.6% growth in a single month is significant: more money chasing the same goods is the textbook recipe for sustained inflation. Even if gas prices cool and headline CPI dips from 4.2%, this accelerating money supply is a structural force that could keep everyday prices elevated through 2027 — complicating any Fed pivot toward cuts.
Inflation
Core PCE Price Index (YoY)
3.4%
ELEVATED →
May 2026 Core PCE — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — held at 3.4% year-over-year (BEA, released June 25), having re-accelerated from a 2.6% cycle low in early 2025. June data will be released in late July. The metric is 1.4 percentage points above the Fed's 2% target and has been climbing for 14 months — the primary reason the FOMC's June dot plot was hawkish. This week's June CPI report (core at 2.6%) is potentially encouraging news: CPI and PCE tend to trend together, and the CPI deceleration suggests June Core PCE may ease from the May reading. If confirmed, it would mark the first genuine turning point in the re-acceleration trend — and reshape the September FOMC calculus.
In plain termsCore PCE is the Fed's preferred inflation yardstick — it strips out food and energy to measure the underlying pace of price increases. At 3.4%, it's well above the 2% target. The Fed has said it needs to see sustained progress toward 2% before cutting rates. This week's June CPI (core at 2.6%) is an encouraging signal — if Core PCE follows the same trend in late July, it could suggest the disinflation that consumers need is finally beginning. For now, borrowing costs stay high until the Fed sees several months of improvement, not just one.
Economic Output
ISM Manufacturing PMI
53.3
EXPANDING ▲
June 2026 ISM Manufacturing PMI came in at 53.3 — down 0.7 percentage points from May's four-year high of 54.0, but the sixth consecutive month of expansion and broadly consistent with roughly 2% real GDP growth on an annualized basis. New orders remained strong at 56.0%, and the Prices Index posted the largest single-month decline since July 2022 (falling 9.1 points to 73%), a genuine disinflation signal from the industrial side of the economy. After bottoming at 47.9 in December 2025, manufacturing has now expanded for six straight months. The modest sequential deceleration reflects sustained momentum, not a reversal.
In plain termsA monthly survey of factory purchasing managers — any reading above 50 means manufacturing is growing, below 50 means it's shrinking. At 53.3, U.S. factories are still expanding at a healthy pace, even if slightly slower than May's four-year high. The good news: factory input prices fell sharply in June, which could eventually translate into lower prices at stores. Six consecutive months of expansion is a genuine recovery signal.
Labor Market
Initial Jobless Claims (4-wk avg)
208k
RECORD LOW ↓↓
The 4-week moving average of initial jobless claims fell to 207,500 for the week ending July 18, 2026 — released July 23 — down 7,250 from the prior week's revised average of 214,750. The weekly print was 187,000 — the lowest single-week reading of 2026 and the fewest initial filings since 1969, roughly 57 years ago. This was a dramatic 22,000 drop from the prior week's 209,000, versus an expectation of 212,000. The data reinforces the sharpest paradox of the current cycle: June payrolls added only 57,000 jobs — but companies are simultaneously retaining workers at a historically unprecedented rate. Layoffs have essentially stopped even as hiring has contracted. Paradoxically, this data strengthens the case for a rate hike: a labor market with near-zero layoffs gives the Fed room to tighten without triggering a recession.
In plain termsEvery week, the government counts how many people filed for unemployment benefits for the first time. This week, just 187,000 people filed — the fewest since 1969. The 4-week average (a smoother measure) fell to 207,500, a new cycle low. If you have a job right now, you're in an unusually secure position — employers are essentially not firing anyone. The flip side: this strength in the labor market actually gives the Fed more justification to hike rates on July 29, since a tight labor market can fuel wage-driven inflation. Hike odds rose to 38% this week, in part because the labor market shows no sign of cracking.

Editor's Analysis

The Reversal: Iran Re-Escalation Triples Hike Odds to 38%, Yields Surge, and Labor Hits a 57-Year Low

FOMC July 29 at 38% hike probability, 10Y yields surge to 4.69%, jobless claims hit 187,000 — lowest since 1969 — By Connor Leary, July 26, 2026

One week ago, this analysis concluded that the worst-case rate hike scenario had "receded significantly." Within seven days, it was back. A renewed escalation of the U.S.-Iran conflict at the Strait of Hormuz — new port attacks, oil prices surging toward $90 per barrel — reversed everything the June CPI report had achieved in a single trading week. CME FedWatch hike odds for the July 29 FOMC meeting tripled, from 10% to 38%, returning the decision to genuine uncertainty. The 10-year Treasury yield rose 14 basis points to 4.69%. The 2-year yield rose 15 basis points to 4.33%. The entire curve shifted materially higher. The July 29 meeting, three days away, is the most consequential and least predictable FOMC decision since the initial Iran shock in March.

The labor market delivered a striking counterweight. Initial jobless claims for the week ending July 18 fell to 187,000 — the fewest single-week filings since 1969. This is not a seasonal quirk; the 4-week moving average fell to 207,500, a new cycle low. The paradox deepens: the same economy that added only 57,000 jobs in June is simultaneously retaining workers at a rate not seen in 57 years. The "slow-hire, no-fire" characterization has evolved into something more extreme. Employers have essentially stopped laying off workers even as the pace of new hiring has contracted sharply. This labor strength actually tilts the Fed calculus toward a hike — a tight labor market generates wage pressure that can sustain services inflation well above the 2% target, regardless of what gasoline prices do.

"From 10% hike odds to 38% in a single week — Iran's return to the Strait of Hormuz has put the July 29 FOMC decision back in play."

The oil channel is the same transmission mechanism that has driven this entire cycle. The Strait of Hormuz crisis that began earlier this year partially unwound after a memorandum of understanding between the U.S. and Iran; June CPI fell to 3.5% as energy prices reversed. Now the attacks have resumed. Analysts at TD Securities cited $100 per barrel as "quite possible" if shipping disruptions deepen. The June CPI's core reading of 2.6% — celebrated last week as the turning point of 2026 — is now at risk of being reversed in the July data. If oil prices remain elevated through July, the headline CPI number due August 12 could retrace toward 4% before the September FOMC even considers cutting rates.

For Chair Warsh, Tuesday's decision pits two competing signals against each other: disinflating core inflation (2.6% core CPI, the lowest since early 2025) versus resurging energy prices, elevated M2 growth (5.6% YoY), and a labor market still too tight by most historical measures. The hawkish dot plot from June — nine of 18 members projecting at least one additional hike — argues that this FOMC does not need a perfect inflation print to act. Warsh's press conference (2:30pm ET) will be parsed for any signal that the board is treating this oil move as transitory, as it did in March, or as a reason to preemptively tighten. Markets are split almost exactly down the middle.

For households, this week's developments are directly consequential. The 10-year Treasury surge from 4.55% to 4.69% has already pushed 30-year fixed mortgage rates from the 7.0–7.2% range to 7.2–7.4% — adding roughly $50 per month to a $400,000 home loan compared to last week. Gasoline prices are rising again at the pump, unwinding June's energy relief. If the Fed hikes Tuesday, credit card rates, auto loans, and HELOCs will reprice upward immediately. One silver lining: with jobless claims at 57-year lows, this is not a labor market under distress. Even in a higher-rate environment, the risk of a sharp consumer spending collapse is low — the household balance sheet is still absorbing rate shock without breaking.
