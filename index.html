Federal Funds Rate
3.63%
HAWKISH HOLD →
The FOMC held the target range at 3.50–3.75% on July 29 (9-3 vote). On August 28, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivered his first major speech as Fed Chair at the Jackson Hole Economic Symposium — and it was unmistakably hawkish. Warsh said inflation "remains stubbornly above" the 2% target, that underlying trends had not "meaningfully improved," and that the Fed "has more work to do." Markets reacted immediately: CME FedWatch shifted from 68% hold / 32% hike to 44% hold / 56% hike for the September 16–17 meeting — making a rate hike the modal expectation for the first time since the July payroll shock. The shift was amplified by the August 26 Core PCE report, which confirmed July Core PCE held at 3.3% YoY — the same as June, rather than the expected decline toward 3.1–3.2%. Two consecutive months of 3.3% Core PCE gave Warsh the data backing to speak hawkishly. The September 5 employment report is now the last major input before the decision.
In plain termsThe Fed kept rates at 3.50–3.75% on July 29, and now markets are pricing a 56% chance of a 25-basis-point hike at the September 16–17 meeting — up sharply from 32% just a week ago. The driver: Fed Chair Warsh gave his first major speech at Jackson Hole on August 28 and said inflation is still too high and rates may need to go higher. The Core PCE report (the Fed's preferred inflation gauge) didn't help matters — July came in at 3.3%, the same as June, not the improvement markets were hoping for. For borrowers, a hike in September would mean credit card APRs above 22%, adjustable-rate mortgages above 7.5%, and higher rates on new car loans. The September 5 jobs report is now the last major data point before the decision — a second payroll loss could override the hawkish impulse; a strong rebound would cement the hike.

Despite the improvement, the Fed is not satisfied: Chair Warsh said at Jackson Hole that inflation is still too high, and Core PCE (the Fed's own preferred gauge) came in flat at 3.3% for July. The softness was broad-based: lower gasoline station receipts (reflecting another monthly drop in pump prices) contributed mechanically, but core measures also showed the payroll shock feeding through. This is the first sign that the July payroll loss (-23,000 jobs) is hitting household budgets: when people lose jobs or fear losing them, they tend to spend less. The 5% YoY figure is still positive (above the ~4.75% long-run average), so this is a slowdown, not a collapse. But the direction matters for the Fed: a weakening consumer alongside easing inflation strengthens the case for a September hold and reduces the chance of a rate hike at the September 16–17 FOMC meeting.

The deceleration from the 5.6% peak in May continues, but the pace of easing remains gradual. The July consumer slowdown (retail sales -0.6% monthly, payrolls -23,000) appears to be translating modestly into slower money creation — but not fast enough to provide meaningful disinflation relief in the near term. Historically, M2 at 5.4% remains well above the 3–4% range associated with 2% inflation. With Core PCE holding at 3.3% in July and Chair Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole signal, the M2 trajectory suggests the structural inflation floor remains intact through at least mid-2027.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money in circulation — cash, bank accounts, money market funds. At 5.4% annual growth (down from 5.5%), the money supply is still expanding faster than the economy produces real goods and services — which is part of why inflation remains above 3% even after coming down from its peak. This excess liquidity is structural: the money created in 2024–2025 is still working through the system with a 12–18 month lag. The slight slowdown from 5.5% to 5.4% is directionally positive but not materially significant — a durable return to 2% inflation likely requires M2 growth to slow toward 3–4%. Until then, upward inflation pressure persists.

Editor's Analysis

Warsh's Warning: Jackson Hole Debut Moves September Hike Odds to 56% as Core PCE Stalls at 3.3%

Core PCE holds at 3.3%, Warsh delivers hawkish Jackson Hole keynote, FOMC odds 44% hold / 56% hike, 2Y Treasury surges to 4.34% — By Connor Leary, August 30, 2026

The week of August 24–28 may be remembered as the moment the September 2026 rate hike became the market's base case. Two events arrived in sequence — and their combined effect was to erase the dovish repricing of the prior week almost entirely. On Wednesday, August 26, the BEA released the July Personal Income and Outlays report. July Core PCE — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — held at 3.3% year-over-year for the second consecutive month. The market had expected a decline toward 3.1–3.2%, consistent with July Core CPI's improvement to 2.5%. The flat reading was not catastrophic, but it was sufficient: two months of 3.3% Core PCE, against a 2% target, gave Chair Kevin Warsh the data footing he needed. On Friday, August 28, Warsh stepped to the podium at the Jackson Hole Economic Symposium for his first major speech as Fed Chair — and he used it.

Warsh's remarks were the clearest hawkish signal from a sitting Fed Chair since the post-COVID tightening cycle. He said inflation "remains stubbornly above" the 2% target, that underlying trends had not "meaningfully improved," and that the Fed "has more work to do." He stopped short of explicitly promising a September hike — but he came as close as a Fed Chair ever does while maintaining plausible deniability. The bond market responded immediately: the 2-year Treasury — which tracks near-term Fed policy expectations most directly — surged from 4.22% before the speech to close at 4.34% (+15 bps on the day). CME FedWatch flipped from 68% hold / 32% hike to 44% hold / 56% hike within hours. Warsh's Jackson Hole debut, combined with the flat Core PCE, produced the single largest one-week shift in September hike probability since the July payroll report sent it in the opposite direction.

"Two months of 3.3% Core PCE gave Warsh the data he needed. Jackson Hole did the rest. The September hike is now the market's base case."

The yield curve dynamics are worth examining carefully. While the 2-year surged 15 basis points, the 10-year ended virtually unchanged at 4.73% — compressing the 2s10s spread from +55 bps to +39 bps. This bear flattening is the textbook signal of a market pricing near-term hikes while keeping long-run growth and inflation expectations anchored. In other words: bond investors believe the Fed will hike in September, but they do not believe the economy is strong enough or inflation persistent enough to require a long series of additional hikes. The market is essentially pricing one more tightening move — which is exactly what the three July dissenters have been calling for. If the September 5 jobs report shows a strong payroll rebound, the curve could flatten further; a second consecutive payroll loss could snap the 2-year back below 4.20% and flip the September outcome overnight.

The flat Core PCE deserves its own analysis. The July reading of 3.3% — unchanged from June — exposed a puzzle that the CPI data had obscured: Core CPI fell from 2.6% to 2.5% in July, yet Core PCE held flat. The divergence reflects methodological differences (PCE weights healthcare and financial services more heavily than CPI) and suggests that while goods disinflation continues, services inflation — the stickier component — has not yet moderated in PCE terms. Headline PCE rose to 3.7% YoY in July, a re-acceleration driven by energy base effects. With Core PCE stuck at 3.3% and the Fed's own preferred metric showing no progress, the disinflationary narrative that supported 68% hold pricing just one week ago has materially weakened. The question is no longer whether September is live — it is — but whether the jobs print is strong enough to deliver the vote.

The September 5 August jobs report is now the most consequential single data point of 2026. After July's -23,000 payroll loss — the first in years — the August report will determine whether that was a one-month anomaly or the beginning of a contraction trend. A second consecutive negative print, even a modest one, would almost certainly preserve the hold: Warsh may want to hike, but a committee voting to tighten into a two-month payroll contraction would face a credibility test of its own. By contrast, a rebound above +100,000 would neutralize the labor market argument for holding, leaving only global growth concerns (China's near-deflationary exports, the World Bank's 2.5% global growth projection) as potential counterweights. Consensus currently expects modest recovery in the 80,000–100,000 range — enough to not be alarming, but perhaps not enough to fully offset July's psychology.

The September 16–17 FOMC meeting is now 17 days away. With CME FedWatch at 56% hike — the highest probability since the pre-July shock — the burden of proof has reversed: it now falls on hold advocates to provide compelling data, rather than on hike advocates to justify action. Three dissenters remain in place from July; Warsh has publicly aligned with their direction without joining their camp; Core PCE has not cooperated; and the yield curve is pricing the move. The remaining swing votes are the September 5 jobs report, the September 10 CPI, and the September 12 retail sales reading. What this means for your wallet: The September 16–17 FOMC is now priced at 44% hold / 56% hike — a dramatic reversal from the 68% hold just a week ago. Fed Chair Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech on August 28 and the flat July Core PCE reading (3.3%, released August 26) together swung the CME FedWatch market decisively toward a September hike. A 25-basis-point hike would push the federal funds target to 3.75–4.00%, the highest since 2024. For your household: credit card APRs (already above 22%) would rise further within one or two billing cycles; adjustable-rate mortgages above 7.5%; car loans above 7.8% for new vehicles. Fixed 30-year mortgage rates would likely move from the current 7.1–7.4% range to 7.4–7.7%. The one remaining lifeline for borrowers: the September 5 August jobs report. A second consecutive payroll loss would give the hold majority powerful cover to override Warsh's hawkish signal — and could send hike odds back below 50% overnight. If you are considering locking in a fixed mortgage rate, the days immediately following the September 5 jobs report may be the best opportunity before the September 16 decision.
