Monetary Policy
Federal Funds Rate
3.88%
HOLD LIKELY: OCT ODDS 17% HIKE →
The FOMC voted 12-0 on September 16 to raise the federal funds target by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00% — the first rate hike since the tightening cycle that ended in 2023, and the highest level since 2024. Three data releases (August Core PCE 3.0%, September ISM Prices Paid 77.9, and September payrolls +29,000 with unemployment 4.2%) drove October 28 hike odds from 76% to 80% hold / 18% hike / 2% cut by October 3. The FOMC minutes from September 15-16 (released October 7) confirmed the unanimous vote and showed most officials still favored another hike "by year end" — but the minutes predate both the Core PCE surprise and the jobs miss, making them effectively stale for October 28 guidance. ISM Services PMI (released October 5) added context: at 54.9 (27th consecutive expansion month), services demand remains solid, but the Prices Index surged to 74.0 — the highest since July 2022. Combined with manufacturing's 77.9 Prices Paid, both ISM surveys are flashing pipeline cost pressure simultaneously. As of October 10, CME FedWatch prices October 27-28 at 82% hold / 17% hike / 1% cut — a modest further shift toward hold. The October 14 CPI (September data) is the last major inflation read before the FOMC decision, followed by Q3 GDP advance estimate (October 29) and September Core PCE (October 30).
In plain termsThe Fed raised rates on September 16 to 3.75–4.00%, but data released over the following two weeks pushed October 28 hike odds from 76% down to just 17%. Core PCE fell to 3.0% (bigger drop than expected), the September jobs report showed only 29,000 new positions, and unemployment ticked up to 4.2%. The FOMC minutes from September 16 (released October 7) confirmed the Fed was still planning another hike — but they were written before the jobs miss and Core PCE drop, so markets largely looked past them. Hold odds have settled at 82% — the highest since the September hike was announced. For your wallet: the 30-year fixed mortgage is still 7.28% (Freddie Mac PMMS, October 1 — the highest of 2026). The 10-year Treasury peaked at 5.36% on October 7 before pulling back to 5.24%. A $400,000 loan costs roughly $2,730 per month. If the Fed holds on October 28 and signals no further hikes, the 10-year could ease back toward 5.0–5.1% and mortgages toward 7.0–7.1%. October 14 CPI is the next major catalyst: a continued cooling confirms the hold; a rebound above 2.6% Core CPI reignites December hike risk.

Consumer Price Index (YoY)
3.4%
MIXED: CORE EASING / ENERGY SURGING ↕
August 2026 CPI held at 3.4% year-over-year (BLS, released September 11) — unchanged from July, leaving headline inflation flat for a second consecutive month and 140 basis points above the Fed's 2% target. Core CPI eased to 2.4% YoY (lowest since March 2021) as shelter decelerated to 3.0% YoY, but energy surged to +16.3% YoY (gasoline +27.4%), offsetting core progress at the headline level. The flat August CPI was a key justification for the FOMC's unanimous September 16 hike to 3.75–4.00%. The August Core PCE that markets had awaited since the delayed September 30 release arrived below every estimate: the Fed's preferred gauge fell to 3.0% — a 30-basis-point decline from two consecutive months at 3.3%. BEA benchmark revisions also lowered July Core PCE from 3.3% to 3.0%. The 90-basis-point divergence between Core CPI (2.4%) and Core PCE has narrowed to 60 basis points as the two measures converge. This PCE improvement — combined with a September jobs miss (+29,000 payrolls, 4.2% unemployment) — drove CME FedWatch October 28 hike odds from 76% to 18%. The next scheduled inflation data is September CPI (October 14) — two weeks before the October 28 FOMC. A Core CPI rebound above 2.6% would reactivate October hike odds; continued disinflation would further cement a hold.
In plain termsInflation held at 3.4% in August — no improvement for the second straight month. The Fed cited this as direct justification for raising rates on September 16. Core CPI (which strips out food and energy) fell to 2.4% — its lowest level since March 2021 — showing the underlying trend is improving. The Fed's own preferred measure, Core PCE, has now also come in better than expected: August Core PCE arrived at 3.0% on September 30, a meaningful improvement from the 3.3% that had been stuck for two months. Gas prices remain up over 16% from a year ago, keeping the headline elevated. For consumers, the 3.4% CPI still means prices are rising faster than the Fed wants, but the improving trend reduces pressure for additional rate hikes. Next read: September CPI on October 14. If it shows continued cooling, the case for a Fed hold on October 28 (already 80% probable) is reinforced.
Labor Market
Unemployment Rate
4.2%
SLOWING: +29K SEP PAYROLLS ↓
The September 2026 employment situation (BLS, released October 2) delivered a significant downside surprise: nonfarm payrolls increased just 29,000 — far below the 84,000 consensus — while the unemployment rate rose to 4.2% from 4.1%. The report was compounded by substantial revisions: July was revised from +21,000 to -10,000 (down 31,000) and August was revised from +162,000 to +133,000 (down 29,000), subtracting a combined 60,000 jobs from previously reported totals. The three-month average for July through September is now +50,667 — well below the 2025 pace and consistent with a meaningfully decelerating labor market. Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1% month-over-month and 3.0% year-over-year — the slowest annual wage growth of 2026 and below current CPI inflation (3.4%), meaning real wages turned negative again in September. The participation rate rose to 61.8% (a four-month high), partially explaining the unemployment rate increase as new entrants searched for work. Initial jobless claims for the week ending September 26 remained historically low at 197,000 weekly (4-week avg: 200,000) — meaning companies are not yet actively laying workers off, but they have materially slowed new hiring. The September payroll weakness — amplified by the -60,000 in prior revisions — drove CME FedWatch's October 28 hike probability from 76% to 18%. The Fed's September 16 hike, made while August's +162,000 was the standing estimate, now appears more aggressive relative to the revised +133,000 figure. The October 2026 jobs report (November 6) will indicate whether September's weakness is an isolated miss or the beginning of a deceleration trend.
In plain termsThe September jobs report shocked markets: only 29,000 new positions were added (vs. 84,000 forecast), unemployment rose to 4.2%, and prior months were revised down a combined 60,000 jobs — July is now officially a monthly loss (-10,000). For workers currently employed, the picture is still stable: only 200,000 people per week are filing for first-time unemployment benefits (near 57-year lows), meaning companies aren't laying off in large numbers. But hiring has slowed sharply. If you're looking for work, expect fewer openings and more competition. Wage growth at 3.0% is now trailing inflation at 3.4%, meaning paychecks are buying slightly less in real terms. The silver lining: this weak report makes a Fed rate hike on October 28 far less likely (18% probability, down from 76% last week). A pause means borrowing costs — including mortgages at 7.28% and credit card APRs above 22% — may represent the near-term ceiling rather than a floor.

Based on available data, Q3 is tracking closer to 1.5–2.0% — similar to Q2 — rather than the 2.0–2.5% range that seemed likely before the jobs report. The primary risk remains the September 16 rate hike to 3.75–4.00%: tighter financial conditions typically weigh on growth with a 6–12 month lag, meaning the full impact will be felt in Q1–Q2 2027. The Q3 2026 advance GDP estimate is expected approximately October 29. Two consecutive negative GDP quarters would constitute a technical recession by common definition (though the NBER uses a broader set of indicators) — Q1 and Q2 both positive (+2.1% and +1.5%) puts that threshold far away, even with Q3 likely to moderate.
In plain termsThe economy grew at +1.5% in Q2 2026 — positive but slower than Q1's +2.1%. Q3 now looks mixed: retail sales were strong in August, factories are still expanding (ISM 54.5), but September added only 29,000 jobs — a weak number — and unemployment rose to 4.2%. Q3 is likely to come in around 1.5–2.0%, similar to Q2 or slightly better. The concern is that the Fed raised rates to 3.75–4.00% in September — and higher rates slow borrowing, spending, and hiring over time with a 6–12 month lag. So while the economy isn't in recession (two consecutive negative quarters would be required, and we're far from that), the deceleration trend from +2.1% to +1.5% and the weak September labor data suggest the economy is losing momentum heading into Q4. The 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.36% on October 7 — a brief new 24-year high, the highest intraday level since October 2002 — driven by heavy Treasury auction supply, elevated fiscal deficit concerns, and the FOMC minutes (released October 7) showing most officials still expected another hike "by year end." By October 9, the 10-year had retreated to 5.24% as the week's data flow (jobless claims still near 57-year lows, softer ISM Services Business Activity) reinforced the hold outlook. The full yield curve as of October 9: 1M=4.13%, 3M=4.16%, 6M=4.32%, 1Y=4.47%, 2Y=4.80%, 5Y=5.02%, 7Y=5.11%, 10Y=5.24%, 20Y=5.64%, 30Y=5.60%. The 2s10s spread remains at +44 bps and the 3m10y narrows to +108 bps (from +118 bps) as the 3-month yield edged higher — the curve is firmly normal and steepening. The 20-year at 5.64% remains the highest point on the curve, a supply/demand technical reflecting Treasury auction composition. Notably, all short rates have declined since October 2 (1M fell from 3.94% to 4.13% — wait, actually that rose; the short end also rose driven by fiscal concerns) while the long end also rose. The entire curve shifted higher 3-8 bps from the Oct 2 reading before the Oct 7 spike and subsequent partial reversal.
In plain termsThe 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.36% on October 7 — its highest level since October 2002 — before settling back to 5.24% by October 9. The spike was driven by heavy government bond supply and the FOMC minutes showing the Fed still leaning toward another hike. The pullback reflects the same forces that have held October hike odds near 82% hold: the September jobs miss and cooling Core PCE. At 5.24%, a $400,000 30-year fixed mortgage still costs roughly $2,730 per month (Freddie Mac PMMS 7.28% as of October 1 — the most recent available reading). If the Fed holds on October 28 (82% probable), the 10-year could ease back toward 5.0–5.1% over the following weeks and mortgages could drift toward 7.0–7.1%. The 2-year Treasury at 4.80% (down from 4.84% on October 2) reflects markets pricing slightly less Fed tightening ahead. The 2s10s spread of +44 bps is unchanged — a sign that long-term growth and inflation expectations are still elevated.
Consumer Activity
Retail Sales Growth (YoY)
6.0%
REBOUNDING STRONGLY ↑
August 2026 retail sales surged +6.0% year-over-year and +1.2% month-over-month (Census Bureau, released September 16) — a decisive reversal of July's -0.6% monthly decline and a significant beat against the 0.8% monthly consensus. Total August retail and food-services sales reached $773.9 billion. The rebound was broad-based: electronics and appliances +7.8% YoY, motor vehicles +0.6% on the month (partly front-loaded ahead of anticipated tariff increases on vehicles), and general merchandise +4.5% YoY. Back-to-school spending drove the gains across apparel and electronics. Released simultaneously with the FOMC's September 16 hike, the August retail data removed any argument for pausing: the American consumer was spending confidently into the tightening cycle. The September jobs report (+29,000 payrolls, unemployment 4.2%) introduces the first sign that consumer activity may be softening: weak hiring tends to dampen spending with a 1–3 month lag. September retail sales (due mid-October) will be the first critical consumer data point of Q4 — a reading below +4.0% YoY would be the first indication the rate channel is biting; above +5.5% would confirm consumer resilience remains intact despite higher borrowing costs.
In plain termsAmericans spent +1.2% more in August than in July — a strong rebound after July's modest decline — and retail sales are now +6.0% above where they were a year ago. This is good news for the economy: consumers are still opening their wallets despite higher prices and borrowing costs. The back-to-school season drove big gains in electronics and clothing. The concern for rate watchers is that strong consumer spending is exactly what keeps inflation alive — and it gives the Fed more reason to hike again at the October 28 meeting. For households, the data suggests the job market and wages are healthy enough to sustain spending, but this also means the Fed is unlikely to reverse course anytime soon. If spending stays strong through Q4, expect rates to remain elevated well into 2027.
Money Supply
M2 Money Supply Growth (YoY)
5.7%
RE-ACCELERATING ↑
M2 grew 5.7% year-over-year through August 2026, per the Federal Reserve's H.6 release published September 22 — an uptick from July's 5.4% and the first acceleration in M2 growth since May's 5.6% local peak. Total M2 now stands at $23.3 trillion. The rebound reverses a three-month moderating trend and arrives at an uncomfortable moment: the Fed hiked to 3.75–4.00% on September 16 specifically to slow money supply growth and tighten financial conditions. The August acceleration reflected the consumer surge (retail sales +6.0% YoY) translating into higher credit card balances and short-term borrowing. The September jobs report's weakness (+29,000 payrolls, unemployment 4.2%) may reduce credit demand going forward — suggesting September and October M2 data could moderate. With October 28 hike odds now at just 18% (down from 76%), the credit-contraction impulse is less aggressive than previously priced. Historically, M2 above 5.5% is incompatible with the Fed's 2% inflation target at any sustained level. The September H.6 (due approximately October 22) will provide the first post-jobs-report read on whether monetary conditions are tightening as intended.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money in the economy — cash, bank accounts, and money market funds. At 5.7% annual growth in August, the money supply re-accelerated after three months of modest easing — unwelcome news in a week when the Fed is already fighting inflation. The jump reflects the August consumer spending surge: people charging more to credit cards and taking short-term loans. Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive, which tends to slow money creation, but that effect takes 6–12 months to show up fully. At 5.7%, M2 remains well above the 3–4% range the Fed would need to see for inflation to reliably return to 2%. For households, rising M2 generally means continued price pressure on everyday goods and services — the monetary brake the Fed is applying needs time to slow this engine down.
Inflation
Core PCE Price Index (YoY)
3.0%
BREAKING LOWER ↓
August 2026 Core PCE — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — fell to 3.0% year-over-year (BEA, Personal Income and Outlays, released September 30), a 30-basis-point decline from two consecutive months at 3.3% and below every major estimate. The downside surprise was compounded by BEA's annual benchmark methodology revision (covering software, legal services, and portfolio fee calculation), which simultaneously lowered July Core PCE from 3.3% to 3.0% — meaning the Fed's preferred gauge has been at 3.0% for two consecutive months under the revised methodology. Headline PCE rose 3.4% YoY in August (with July revised from 3.7% to 3.4% under the same benchmark changes). The 30-basis-point decline in Core PCE was the single most market-moving data point of the week, driving CME FedWatch's October 28 hike probability from 76% to 18% within hours of the release. The 90-basis-point gap between Core CPI (2.4%) and Core PCE has narrowed to 60 basis points — the two measures are converging, which removes a key argument that PCE was structurally elevated beyond what CPI data suggested. At 3.0%, Core PCE is now 100 basis points above the Fed's 2% target, down from 130 basis points. Combined with the September jobs miss (+29,000 payrolls, unemployment 4.2%), the 3.0% Core PCE reading shifts the balance firmly toward a hold at the October 27–28 FOMC. The September Core PCE (due approximately October 30, BEA) will indicate whether the improvement is sustained.
In plain termsCore PCE is the Fed's preferred inflation measure — and it dropped to 3.0% in August, a bigger improvement than expected. The BEA also revised July's reading down from 3.3% to 3.0%, meaning inflation has been running cooler than previously known. At 3.0%, Core PCE is still 100 basis points above the Fed's 2% target — prices are still rising 50% faster than the Fed wants. But the direction has changed decisively: this is the first meaningful drop in Core PCE after two flat months at 3.3%, and it was the primary reason October 28 hike odds collapsed from 76% to 18% within a single day. For consumers: every $50 in spending is going up by about $1.50 per year at 3.0% — still uncomfortable, but down from $1.65 at 3.3%. The 2% target would mean only $1 per $50. The Fed's preferred gauge is moving in the right direction; if it continues to fall, borrowing costs (mortgages, credit cards, car loans) could begin to ease in 2027. The next reading arrives around October 30.

ISM Manufacturing PMI
54.5
9TH MONTH EXPANDING ↓
September 2026 ISM Manufacturing PMI registered 54.5 (ISM, released October 1) — down 0.1 point from August's 54.6 and the ninth consecutive month of expansion. New orders: 53.2; employment sub-index: 50.8 (still expansionary but the softest since February); production: 57.1. The headline reading was largely steady, but the standout sub-index was Prices Paid, which surged to 77.9 — the highest reading since early 2022 and up from August's 71.3. A Prices Paid reading above 70 historically precedes consumer goods inflation 3–6 months forward, which adds a complicating layer for the Fed's path to 2% Core PCE. Nine consecutive expansion months is notable: manufacturing has absorbed the full Fed tightening cycle without a single contraction. The employment sub-index fall to 50.8 is directionally consistent with the September jobs report's 29,000 payroll miss and 4.2% unemployment rate — both pointing to a labor market that is decelerating without collapsing. With FOMC hold odds at 80% following the August Core PCE drop to 3.0% and the September jobs miss, the manufacturing sector's steady PMI provides a soft-landing argument. October ISM PMI (due November 3) will be the first post-October-28 FOMC read on manufacturing momentum.
In plain termsAny ISM reading above 50 means manufacturing is expanding; below 50 means it's contracting. At 54.5, U.S. factories are in solid growth for the ninth consecutive month — still-healthy new orders (53.2), positive employment (50.8), and strong production (57.1). The concern is Prices Paid, which surged to 77.9 — the highest since early 2022. When manufacturers pay more for inputs, prices eventually pass through to consumers. This is a warning signal that some of the inflation improvement seen in Core PCE (down to 3.0%) could face renewed pressure from factory costs in late 2026 and early 2027. For workers in manufacturing: nine consecutive expansion months means continued job security, but the employment sub-index is softening toward neutral. For rate watchers: the Prices Paid surge makes a rate cut in 2027 harder to justify, even as the weak jobs report and lower PCE argue for a hold at October 28.

NEAR 57-YEAR LOWS ↓
Initial jobless claims for the week ending October 3 came in at 197,000 (DOL, released October 8) — unchanged from the prior week’s 197,000, with the prior week revised up from 197,000 to 199,000. The 4-week moving average declined to 198,000, down 2,500 from the prior week. Now into the 8th consecutive week near 57-year lows, the claims data continues to describe a labor market in which employers are retaining workers even as hiring has collapsed. The September jobs report (+29,000 payrolls on October 2) showed net hiring at a 14-month low; claims for week ending October 3 confirm that mass layoffs remain absent. This is the first week of Q4 2026 labor market data — and it shows no deterioration. ISM Services employment (released October 5) returned to expansion at 50.1 (up from 47.8 in August), corroborating the layoff signal. Historically, ultra-low claims alongside weak payrolls tend to persist until one of two catalysts triggers a break: either companies resume hiring (softlanding) or a credit or demand shock triggers an abrupt rise in layoffs (hard landing). Neither catalyst is visible in the current data. For the FOMC: claims at 198,000 reinforce the hold case — the labor market is not in distress, reducing urgency for emergency easing, while the weak payroll print argues against additional tightening.
In plain termsFor the week ending October 3 — the first week of Q4 2026 — only 197,000 people filed for unemployment benefits. The 4-week average is now 198,000, near 57-year lows for eight straight weeks. This means very few workers are being laid off. But September’s jobs report (just 29,000 new jobs) showed that companies aren’t hiring either. The paradox continues: secure if you have a job, harder to find a new one. For rate watchers: ultra-low claims reduce the urgency for an emergency rate cut, while weak payrolls argue against hiking further. Both signals point directly toward a hold on October 28 — now priced at 82% odds. For the Fed, the ideal soft-landing scenario would be claims staying low while payrolls gradually rebound from September’s 29,000 miss. The October jobs report (due November 6) is the next read on whether September was an isolated miss or a trend.

Editor's Analysis

Yield Surge and Retreat: The 10-Year Hits a 24-Year High Before Pulling Back, as Two ISM Price Indexes at Multi-Year Highs Complicate the Disinflation Narrative

10Y peaked 5.36% Oct 7 (24-yr high), settled 5.24%; ISM Services Prices 74.0 (Jul '22 high); jobless claims 197K/198K avg; FOMC hold odds 82%; Oct 14 CPI next catalyst — Written by AI agents designed and managed by Connor Leary, October 11, 2026

The 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.36% intraday on October 7 — its highest level since October 2002 — before retreating to 5.24% by October 9. The October 7 spike was driven by a confluence of supply and sentiment factors: heavy Treasury auction demand was soft, fiscal deficit concerns re-emerged as Congress approached a debt ceiling standoff, and the FOMC minutes released that same afternoon confirmed that most officials at the September 16 meeting believed another hike "would likely be appropriate by year end." The minutes — which reflected the policy view of mid-September, written before August Core PCE fell to 3.0% and before September payrolls came in at just 29,000 — were immediately flagged by bond markets as stale. October 28 hike odds barely moved on the minutes, staying near 17–18%; the long end was responding to structural supply factors, not near-term Fed re-pricing. By October 9, the 10-year had retraced to 5.24%, a level that still reflects elevated real rates and a structurally higher long-end environment.

ISM Services PMI for September (released October 5) reinforced an uncomfortable duality in the economic data. The headline reading of 54.9 was the 27th consecutive month of expansion — solid, broad-based activity data with Business Activity at 56.5 and New Orders at 59.8. Employment returned to expansion at 50.1, up sharply from August's 47.8. But the Prices Index surged to 74.0 — the highest since July 2022 and up 1.4 points from August's 72.6. Combined with ISM Manufacturing's Prices Paid at 77.9 (released October 1, the highest since early 2022), both major economic sectors are now simultaneously reporting the most elevated input cost pressures in four-plus years. A Prices Index above 70 in either ISM survey has historically transmitted to consumer price indices within 3–6 months, which places the risk window squarely in Q1 2027. This is the central tension in the current macro environment: downstream consumer prices are cooling (Core PCE 3.0%, Core CPI 2.4%), while upstream pipeline pressures are building.

"Two ISM price indexes above 70 simultaneously — manufacturing at 77.9 and services at 74.0 — is the pipeline pressure gauge that will determine whether the disinflation story holds or re-inflects in early 2027."

The labor market's internal contradiction persists. For the week ending October 3 (released October 8), initial jobless claims held at 197,000 — the fourth consecutive week at or below 200,000 — and the 4-week average declined to 198,000 from 200,000. Near 57-year lows for eight consecutive weeks, claims describe a workforce that remains employed but is not growing. ISM Services employment at 50.1 adds corroborating evidence on the services side. The September jobs report (+29,000 payrolls, unemployment 4.2%) established that net hiring has collapsed; claims confirm that net firing has not followed. Historically, this divergence — minimal layoffs alongside near-stagnant hiring — persists for 2–5 months before resolving into either a hiring recovery or an abrupt rise in layoffs. The October jobs report (November 6) will be the first test of which path is unfolding.

The question now is whether October 14's CPI print will reinforce the disinflation narrative that drove the October 28 hold consensus, or introduce doubt. August Core CPI was 2.4% — its lowest since March 2021. The ISM price signals (services 74.0, manufacturing 77.9) argue for upside risk to September CPI. Shelter inflation (3.0% in August) has been gradually easing but remains well above its pre-pandemic norm. Energy at +16.3% YoY in August remains the dominant headline distortion. A September Core CPI above 2.6% — even without a corresponding spike in headline — would reignite the 17% hike probability and shift December back into play. A reading at or below 2.4% would further cement the hold and begin the conversation about when cuts become appropriate.

The ten days from October 28 through October 30 represent the most concentrated data sequence of the year. The FOMC decision on October 28 (hold at 82% probability) will be followed by the Q3 2026 advance GDP estimate on October 29 and September Core PCE on October 30. For Chair Warsh, the communications challenge is acute: the September 16 dot plot called for a 4.10% year-end median rate — implicitly endorsing one more hike. Holding on October 28 — while leaving the dot plot unchanged at 4.10% — threads a difficult needle: signaling neither victory on inflation nor a definitive end to tightening. The alternative — revising the median down to 3.875% — would declare the terminal rate reached and risk an immediate rally in risk assets that loosens financial conditions. Markets will watch the dot plot as closely as the rate decision itself.

For households, the 30-year fixed mortgage is 7.28% as of October 1 (Freddie Mac PMMS, the most recent reading) — unchanged from the prior week. The 10-year Treasury peaking at 5.36% on October 7 and pulling back to 5.24% by October 9 suggests mortgage rates may be near a ceiling rather than still rising. A hold on October 28 accompanied by dovish dot plot revisions — or a benign September Core PCE on October 30 — could ease the 10-year toward 5.0–5.1% over the following weeks and bring 30-year mortgages back toward 7.0–7.1%. A $400,000 home loan at 7.28% costs approximately $2,730 per month; at 7.0%, it falls to about $2,661. For savers: short-term Treasury yields at 4.13% (1-month) and 4.80% (2-year) remain significantly above Core PCE (3.0%), preserving the real return advantage of cash over the near term.
