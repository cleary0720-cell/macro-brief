The downstream data released since the advance estimate has been uniformly softer: July nonfarm payrolls -23,000 (the first job loss in years) and July retail sales +5.0% YoY (down sharply from June's 6.7%) both point to Q3 growth tracking below the Q2 pace. In plain termsThe economy grew at a +1.5% pace in Q2 2026 — still positive, but below Q1's +2.1%. This is a yellow flag: the economy is slowing, not collapsing. But data released since then has painted a more cautious picture: the July jobs report showed the first payroll loss in years, and now July retail sales show consumers spending less for the first time in months. A recession technically requires two consecutive quarters of negative growth, and Q2 is still positive. Q3 GDP won't be reported until late October — but the trajectory of the incoming data makes it the most-watched estimate since the tariff-driven Q1 2025 contraction.

Consumer Activity
Retail Sales Growth (YoY)
5.0%
MODERATING ↓
July 2026 advance retail sales came in at +5.0% year-over-year — down sharply from June's 6.7% and the weakest annual reading since early 2026. On a monthly basis, sales fell 0.6% from June to July, the first meaningful monthly decline in six months. The softness was broad-based: lower gasoline station receipts (reflecting another monthly drop in pump prices) contributed mechanically, but core measures also showed the payroll shock feeding through. Total sales for May through July are up 6.3% from a year ago — still above the ~4.75% long-run average, but the trajectory has shifted decisively. This is the first hard evidence that July's 23,000 payroll loss and prior-month downward revisions of 146,000 are translating into reduced consumer spending — the consumer had been the primary counterweight to labor market weakness through June.
In plain termsAmericans spent 0.6% less in July than in June — the first real pullback in months — and year-over-year retail growth slowed sharply from 6.7% to 5.0%. This is the first sign that the July payroll loss (-23,000 jobs) is hitting household budgets: when people lose jobs or fear losing them, they tend to spend less. The 5% YoY figure is still positive (above the ~4.75% long-run average), so this is a slowdown, not a collapse. But the direction matters for the Fed: a weakening consumer alongside easing inflation strengthens the case for a September hold and reduces the chance of a rate hike at the September 16–17 FOMC meeting.
Money Supply
M2 Money Supply Growth (YoY)
5.5%
ELEVATED →
M2 grew 5.5% year-over-year through June 2026, per the Federal Reserve's H.6 release published July 28 — a slight moderation from May's 5.6%. Total M2 now stands at $23.16 trillion. The July 2026 H.6 data is expected around late August and will provide the first read on whether July's payroll weakness and consumer pullback have begun to slow money supply growth. Despite the minor monthly easing, M2 remains at elevated levels that historically precede inflation by 12–18 months. The two-month CPI improvement (from 4.2% in May to 3.4% in July) is directionally positive, but the structural money supply overhang continues to provide a floor for inflationary pressure well into 2027. A durable return to 2% inflation likely requires M2 growth falling toward 3–4%.
In plain termsM2 measures all the money in circulation — cash, bank accounts, money market funds. At 5.5% growth, there is still more money in the economy than the real output justifies. This excess liquidity is why inflation — even as it eases from 4.2% to 3.4% — remains above 3%: there is simply too much money chasing goods and services. Historically, elevated M2 growth feeds into consumer prices with a 12–18 month lag, meaning the money pumped into the economy in late 2025 continues to exert upward pressure on prices now. The July H.6 (expected late August) will offer the next read on whether the consumer slowdown is translating into slower money supply growth.

In plain termsAny ISM reading above 50 means manufacturing is growing; below 50 means shrinking. At 55.6, U.S. factories are expanding at their fastest pace in four years, and manufacturers added workers in July for the first time in nearly three years. This is genuinely good news — manufacturing jobs tend to pay well, and strong factory output supports the broader economy. The friction: consumer spending is now pulling back (-0.6% monthly in July), raising questions about whether goods demand can sustain the factory expansion over the next few months. For the Fed, a booming factory sector gives the three hawkish dissenters an argument: the economy isn't breaking, so why not hike? August data (due Sep 2) will test whether the expansion continues.

Editor's Analysis

Between Minutes and Jackson Hole: Markets Price 68% Hold as Warsh Prepares His First Major Speech and Core PCE Looms Wednesday

FOMC Minutes hawkish but market dovishly repriced, FOMC odds 68% hold / 32% hike, 10Y Treasury 4.74%, jobless claims 4-wk avg 204k — By Connor Leary, August 23, 2026

The most eventful week of the pre-September window arrived and departed with the market more confident about a hold — but not by much. On August 19, the Federal Reserve released the minutes of its July 28–29 FOMC meeting. The document was unambiguously hawkish in tone: it revealed that "several" members beyond the three official dissenters (Hammack, Kashkari, Logan) considered raising rates at July's meeting, and that the committee broadly coalesced around the view that "policy tightening would likely be necessary if inflation did not decline." Paradoxically, the hawkish minutes triggered a dovish market repricing. CME FedWatch shifted from 52% hold / 48% hike to 68% hold / 32% hike — a 16-point swing — as investors concluded that the deteriorating labor market backdrop (July payrolls -23,000, jobless claims 4-week average rising from 199,000 to 204,000) would prevent the majority from crossing over to the hawkish camp by September 16.

The minutes' hawkish content is worth taking seriously on its own terms. The July meeting was not a close call that happened to resolve in a hold; it was a committee broadly oriented toward tightening that chose to wait one more meeting for confirming data. Three dissenters openly voted for an immediate 25-bps hike — the first three-way dissent in the same direction since September 2016. The majority's hold was conditional, not philosophical: it was premised on the expectation that July CPI (3.4%, released August 12) and July retail sales (+5.0% YoY, released August 14) would produce data soft enough to justify patience. For the September meeting, the threshold is similar: the majority needs the August 27 Core PCE and the September 5 jobs report to affirm that the disinflationary trend is intact and the labor market remains under pressure. If those two data points surprise to the hawkish side, the September hold consensus collapses quickly.

"Hawkish minutes, dovish repricing — the market believes the data will do the Fed's tightening for it. This week's Core PCE will test that conviction."

The most important number of the week arrives on Wednesday, August 27. The BEA's Personal Income and Outlays report will contain July Core PCE — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, which stood at 3.3% YoY in June. Market consensus expects a decline toward 3.1–3.2%, consistent with July's core CPI improvement to 2.5%. A sub-3.1% reading would dramatically strengthen the hold thesis: it would suggest that the disinflationary path telegraphed by CPI is being corroborated in PCE, and that the gap to the 2% target is narrowing at a pace that makes a September hike increasingly hard to defend. A reading above 3.3% would do the opposite — reigniting hike expectations, reversing the dovish FedWatch repricing, and handing the three dissenters an additional data point to bring wavering colleagues across the aisle.

Also on the calendar this week: Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first major speech as Fed Chair at the Jackson Hole Economic Symposium on Friday, August 28. His address — the first from a new Fed Chair at Jackson Hole since Powell's 2018 debut — arrives in circumstances as fraught as any in recent memory: a fractured committee, a coin-flip September decision, and a labor market that produced negative payrolls for the first time in years just three weeks ago. Warsh has indicated he may focus on long-term structural questions rather than near-term policy signals, and 69% of fund managers surveyed by BofA expect a neutral tone. But in the current environment, even structural rhetoric will be parsed for September signals. Markets will key on any language about the labor market's role in the inflation calculus, and on whether Warsh characterizes the hold majority as data-dependent or definitively patient.

Treasury yields drifted modestly higher this week despite the dovish FedWatch repricing — a divergence worth noting. The 10-year Treasury ended the week at 4.74% (up 4 bps from August 14's 4.70%), and the 2s10s spread widened to +55 bps. The yield backup reflects two competing forces: the dovish September repricing should have pulled yields lower, but Treasury Secretary Bessent's announcement of an accelerated bond buyback program introduced a new supply-demand variable that temporarily spiked yields before they settled slightly higher. For mortgage borrowers, 30-year fixed rates remain in the 7.1–7.4% range. The path to meaningful relief is straightforward but binary: a soft August 27 Core PCE combined with a neutral-to-dovish Warsh speech could pull the 10-year below 4.60% and test 7.0% mortgage rates within two weeks. A hot PCE combined with a hawkish Warsh could push yields back toward 4.80–4.90%, closing the year on a restrictive note.

The September 16–17 FOMC meeting is now 24 days away. The FedWatch market is pricing it at 68% hold — the strongest hold probability since the dissent-riven July meeting — but the three weeks between now and the decision remain the most data-intensive of the cycle. Core PCE (Wednesday), Warsh's remarks (Friday), and the September 5 jobs report all have genuine potential to move the needle 10–20 percentage points in either direction. Three dissenters represent the most organized hawkish faction within the FOMC in a decade, and they are actively recruiting additional votes. The majority that held in July did so on the basis of conditional data expectations that are still being tested. The market's growing confidence in a September hold should be read as a probabilistic assessment, not a verdict — the next five days alone could change everything.

What this means for your wallet: The September 16–17 FOMC is now priced at 68% hold / 32% hike — a meaningful improvement for borrowers compared to last week's 52/48 split. The August 19 FOMC minutes showed the Fed was broadly leaning toward tightening in July, but the softening consumer backdrop (payrolls -23,000, retail sales -0.6% monthly, jobless claims rising) convinced markets that the majority will wait. For your mortgage, car loan, or credit card APR: a hold in September keeps borrowing costs unchanged from today's already-elevated levels. A hike would add 25 bps to variable-rate products — credit card APRs above 22%, adjustable-rate mortgages above 7.5%. The two decisive data points: August 27 Core PCE (this Wednesday — if it falls from 3.3% toward 3.0%, hold odds could rise toward 80%+) and September 5 August Jobs Report (a second payroll loss makes a hold near-certain; a rebound above +100k could push hike odds back above 50%). Chair Warsh's Jackson Hole speech on Friday, August 28 is a wildcard: if he signals September is genuinely on the table, expect an immediate yield spike and tighter borrowing costs. For anyone considering locking in a mortgage rate, the Wednesday-morning window after the Core PCE release — before any Jackson Hole repricing — may offer the best near-term rate environment of 2026.
